    15→In previous grades, we have studied the history of various kingdoms 
    16→and empires, and also learned about the Sindhu–Sarasvatī 
    17→civilisation. However, the history of humanity goes back much 
    18→further. In this chapter, we will explore who our earliest ancestors 
    19→were, when and  where they evolved, what kinds of food they ate, 
    20→how they lived, and how they became what we are today.
    21→The period before the development of writing is understood mainly 
    22→through archaeological evidence, as written languages emerged at 
    23→different times in different parts of the world. 
    24→One of the earliest known writing systems is associated with the 
    25→Sindhu–Sarasvatī, also known as the Indus Valley Civilisation or the 
    26→Harappan Civilisation. Writings and engravings found on objects such 
    27→as seals and pottery, indicate that the Harappans used a pictographic 
    28→script for writing. However, it has not yet been deciphered. As a 
    29→result, we still do not understand the Harappan script, also referred 
    30→to as Sindhu lipi.
    31→Other early writing systems include the cuneiform script of 
    32→the Sumerians in Mesopotamia and the hieroglyphic script of 
    33→ancient Egypt, both of which flourished around the same time as 
    34→the Harappan Civilisation. Unlike the Harappan script, these scripts 
    35→have been deciphered and mark the beginning of the historical 
    36→period, about 5000 years ago. You will learn more about them later 
    37→in the chapter.

   212→Who Were Our Human Ancestors?
   213→The earliest human settlements were found in Africa, Asia, and 
   214→Europe, and together they constitute what we today call the ‘Old 
   215→World’. Different kinds of human ancestors co-existed at this time 
   216→(see Fig. 4.6), and it was around 3.3 million years ago, that one of these 
   217→ancestors made the earliest stone tools. This marked the beginning 
   218→of the so-called ‘human behaviour’, as opposed to the behaviour 
   219→of animals, who do not possess the cognitive ability to make such 
   220→tools. Thus, human beings came to be known as ‘hominins’, or tool 
   221→makers. The tools they created are sometimes called extra-corporal 
   222→limbs because they functioned as extensions of the human body, 
   223→helping people perform tasks they could not accomplish with their 
   224→hands alone. Humans made these tools, used them, and discarded 
   225→them when they wore out. Over time, these discarded tools got

   570→The Neolithic Revolution
   571→As hunter–gatherers gained adequate familiarity with seasons and 
   572→different types of food resources, there was a gradual transition to a 
   573→food-producing way of life, also known as the Neolithic revolution. 
   574→The hallmark of this revolution was the domestication of select 
   575→animals and plants, bringing them under human control, and the 
   576→development of new breeds through cultivation and husbandry. 
   577→While hunter–gatherers produced tools for procuring food, Neolithic 
   578→farmers made tools for food production and processing (Fig. 4.13) 
   579→and developed a variety of earthenware pottery in various shapes 
   580→and sizes. They utilised several raw materials and resources and 
   581→established the first village settlements, laying the foundations for 
   582→the urban revolution.

   584→gathering to agricultural ways of life across different regions of the 
   585→world. It also highlights that this transition did not occur at the same 
   586→time everywhere, but took place at different periods in different 
   587→areas.

   669→Neolithic Period in Indian Subcontinent
   670→In the Indian subcontinent, we find regional variation in the initiation 
   671→of agricultural ways of life. In the northwest, the site of Mehrgarh 
   672→on the Bolan River (in present-day Pakistan) is the oldest Neolithic 
   673→site. It was also the earliest agricultural village, dating back to about 
   674→7000 BCE. Its people built handmade sun-dried brick houses and 
   675→granaries, buried their dead in graves, 
   676→and made a variety of ornaments from 
   677→semi-precious stones, such as lapis lazuli, 
   678→carnelian, and shells. They cultivated 
   679→wheat and barley, and raised sheep, goats, 
   680→and Indian cattle—the zebu humped bull 
   681→in particular. They were also the first to 
   682→make copper objects, thus entering the 
   683→metal age and becoming popularly known 
   684→as the Chalcolithic people by about 4000 
   685→BCE. This laid the basis for the Bronze 
   686→Age Sindhu–Sarasvatī civilisation around 
   687→3500 BCE.
