   237→  <div class="topic-h"><span class="tnum">T1</span><span class="tt">Economics — What is it? 🤔</span><span class="tick">☐ revised</span></div>
   238→  <div class="define"><div class="dl">📖 Definition</div><div class="dd"><b>Economics</b> is the discipline that deals with how choices are made by <b>optimising (best use of)</b> limited resources to satisfy the <b>needs and wants</b> of people.</div></div>
   239→  <div class="hing"><div class="ht">💡 Samajh (Hinglish)</div><div class="hb">Word aaya hai Greek se — <b>oikonomia</b> = <b>oikos</b> (household) + <b>nemein</b> (management) = <b>"household management"</b>. Simple: jaise ghar me mummy paise limited hote hain aur unse poora month chalati hain — wahi economics hai, bas poore desh ke level pe! Government, companies, families — sabko yahi problem hoti hai: <b>kam resources, zyada demands</b>.</div></div>
   240→  <div class="plabel">✍️ Exam Points — yehi likhna hai</div>
   241→  <ul class="pts">
   242→    <li><b>Origin:</b> Greek word <b>oikonomia</b> — oikos (household) + nemein (management), meaning 'household management'.</li>
   243→    <li>Economics studies how <b>individuals, enterprises and governments</b> make choices to use limited resources efficiently.</li>
   244→    <li>It explains how <b>economic entities</b> — consumers, producers, governments, financial institutions — interact in an economy.</li>
   245→    <li>It covers: how people earn wages, how wealth is distributed, how prices are determined, how policies &amp; trade affect employment.</li>
   246→    <li>Good economic decisions are based on <b>data and analysis</b> — not guesswork (data = facts &amp; statistics; surveys = systematic data collection).</li>
   247→  </ul>

   251→<div class="topic">
   252→  <div class="topic-h"><span class="tnum">T2</span><span class="tt">Needs vs Wants 🍚 vs 🎮</span><span class="tick">☐ revised</span></div>
   253→  <div class="define"><div class="dl">📖 Definitions</div><div class="dd"><b>Needs</b> — essentials required for survival, like food, water and shelter. &nbsp;|&nbsp; <b>Wants</b> — desires beyond essentials, like gadgets, vacations and luxury items.</div></div>
   254→  <div class="hing"><div class="ht">💡 Samajh (Hinglish)</div><div class="hb"><b>Need</b> = bina ye zinda nahi reh sakte (khana, paani, ghar). <b>Want</b> = dil ki demand — cycle se bike, bike se car, car se... aur badhte hi jao! 🚲→🏍️→🚗 Isliye kehte hain <b>"human wants are unlimited and keep changing"</b> — yahi economics ki root problem hai.</div></div>
   255→  <div class="plabel">✍️ Exam Points</div>
   256→  <ul class="pts">
   257→    <li><b>Needs</b> are essential for survival — food, water, shelter.</li>
   258→    <li><b>Wants</b> are unlimited, keep changing, and grow with time — e.g., bicycle → motorbike → car.</li>
   259→    <li>Choices are made by individuals, enterprises and governments — e.g., spending pocket money on snacks vs saving for shoes.</li>
   260→    <li>Since wants exceed resources, <b>every economic choice involves giving something up</b>.</li>
   261→  </ul>
   262→  <div class="exbox"><span class="xt">🧠 Quick trick:</span> Need = <b>N</b>ahi hua toh <b>N</b>ahi bachoge. Want = <b>W</b>ish list — hoga toh mast, nahi toh chalega!</div>

   267→  <div class="topic-h"><span class="tnum">T3</span><span class="tt">Scarcity &amp; Resources — The Root Problem 🥲</span><span class="tick">☐ revised</span></div>
   268→  <div class="define"><div class="dl">📖 Definitions</div><div class="dd"><b>Resources</b> — factors used for the production of goods and services; they can be natural (water, coal) or human-made (capital, technology). &nbsp;|&nbsp; <b>Scarcity</b> — the condition of limited resources against unlimited wants.</div></div>
   269→  <div class="hing"><div class="ht">💡 Samajh (Hinglish)</div><div class="hb">Ek hi <b>steel</b> ka block — usse hospital ke <b>medical equipment</b> bana sakte ho, <b>fridge</b> bhi, <b>aircraft</b> bhi. Steel kam hai, uses bahut saare — matlab resources ki <b>alternative uses</b> hain. Toh economy ko decide karna padta hai kahan lagana hai. Yahi <b>scarcity</b> hai — aur isi se <b>choice</b> ka problem paida hota hai.</div></div>
   270→  <div class="plabel">✍️ Exam Points</div>
   271→  <ul class="pts">
   272→    <li>Both natural and human-made resources are <b>limited in quantity</b>.</li>
   273→    <li>Resources have <b>many alternative uses</b> — e.g., steel can make medical equipment, refrigerators or aircraft.</li>
   274→    <li><b>Factors of production</b> (Grade 8 recap): <b>Land, Labour, Capital, Technology</b>.</li>
   275→    <li>Not just households — whole <b>economies</b> must decide how to use scarce resources to improve people's quality of life.</li>
   276→    <li>Scarcity + unlimited wants → <b>choices</b> → every choice has a cost (next topic!).</li>
   277→  </ul>

   281→<div class="topic">
   282→  <div class="topic-h"><span class="tnum">T4</span><span class="tt">Opportunity Cost — What You Give Up 💸</span><span class="tick">☐ revised</span></div>
   283→  <div class="define"><div class="dl">📖 Definition</div><div class="dd"><b>Opportunity cost</b> — the <b>value of what is given up</b> when one alternative is chosen and the other options are sacrificed.</div></div>
   284→  <div class="hing"><div class="ht">💡 Samajh (Hinglish)</div><div class="hb">Friday evening: movie <b>ya</b> cricket match — dono ek saath nahi. Movie chuni toh <b>match ka maza</b> gaya = movie ka opportunity cost. Book example: farmer ke paas ek piece of land — usme <b>barley ya wheat</b> ugaa sakta hai. Barley boya toh wheat ka sacrifice = barley ka opportunity cost. Yaad rakhna: <b>jo chhoda, wahi cost hai!</b></div></div>
   285→  <div class="plabel">✍️ Exam Points</div>
   286→  <ul class="pts">
   287→    <li>When one alternative is chosen, the other options are <b>given up</b> — their value is the opportunity cost.</li>
   288→    <li><b>Farmer example:</b> with limited land, water and labour, he can grow barley or wheat in different combinations — more barley means less wheat.</li>
   289→    <li>Opportunity cost applies to <b>households, enterprises and governments</b> alike (e.g., govt: highways vs hospitals).</li>
   290→    <li>Understanding it improves decision-making — we compare what we gain against what we sacrifice.</li>
   291→  </ul>
   292→  <div class="exbox"><span class="xt">🧠 Exam line:</span> "Every economic choice has an opportunity cost" — 1-mark question me seedha likh do. ✔️</div>

   297→  <div class="topic-h"><span class="tnum">T5</span><span class="tt">Production Possibility Curve (PPC) 📉</span><span class="tick">☐ revised</span></div>
   298→  <div class="define"><div class="dl">📖 Definition</div><div class="dd"><b>PPC</b> — the curve showing <b>different combinations of two goods</b> that can be produced using <b>all available resources</b> efficiently.</div></div>
   299→  <div class="hing"><div class="ht">💡 Samajh (Hinglish)</div><div class="hb">PPC ek <b>seesaw</b> jaisa hai — ek side barley, doosri side wheat. Ek side ka weight badhaya toh doosri girti hai! Graph me x-axis pe <b>barley (kg)</b>, y-axis pe <b>wheat (kg)</b>, aur curve <b>neeche ki taraf jhuki (downward sloping)</b> hoti hai — kyunki zyada barley chahiye toh wheat sacrifice karni padegi. <b>A se E</b> tak move karo → barley badhti hai, wheat ghat-ti jaati hai.</div></div>
   300→  <div class="plabel">✍️ Exam Points</div>
   301→  <ul class="pts">
   302→    <li>PPC shows the <b>trade-off</b> between two goods (here: barley and wheat).</li>
   303→    <li>It is <b>downward sloping</b> — producing more of one good requires sacrificing some of the other.</li>
   304→    <li><b>Points ON the curve</b> = maximum output with <b>efficient use of resources</b> (no wastage).</li>
   305→    <li>It helps <b>enterprises and governments</b> in better planning and decision-making.</li>
   306→  </ul>

   358→  <div class="topic-h"><span class="tnum">T9</span><span class="tt">Economic Systems — 3 Tarika 🏛️</span><span class="tick">☐ revised</span></div>
   359→  <div class="define"><div class="dl">📖 Definition</div><div class="dd"><b>Economic system</b> — the system that defines the mechanisms for the <b>production, consumption and distribution</b> of goods, services and resources in a country.</div></div>
   360→  <div class="hing"><div class="ht">💡 Samajh (Hinglish)</div><div class="hb">3 systems = 3 personalities 😄 — <b>Planned</b>: "Sarkar sab decide karegi" (strict papa 🧔). <b>Market</b>: "Demand-supply khud decide karega, sarkar bas referee" (cool referee 🏃). <b>Mixed</b>: "Dono mil ke" — India wala practical combo (office me dono bosses 👨‍💼👩‍💼). Har system wahi 3 questions (kya/kaise/kiske liye) alag tarah se answer karta hai.</div></div>
   361→  <div class="plabel">✍️ Exam Points — the 3 systems</div>
   362→  <ul class="pts">
   363→    <li><b>Planned economy:</b> a <b>central planning authority</b> of the government (like a planning commission) makes all major decisions — what, how much, how, for whom and at what prices. Government owns most resources (land, factories, banks, transport). Strict <b>permits &amp; licenses</b> → little competition → less motivation to improve quality or innovate. <b>Examples: former Soviet Union, North Korea, Cuba.</b></li>
   364→    <li><b>Market economy:</b> decisions are made mainly by <b>forces of demand and supply</b> with little government intervention — government acts like a <b>referee</b> (safety, law &amp; order; does not control prices or production). Ownership largely with <b>individuals &amp; private companies</b>. Competition → better quality, lower prices, innovation. <b>Examples: USA, Japan, Hong Kong.</b></li>
   365→    <li><b>Mixed economy:</b> combines features of both — private individuals, enterprises AND the government play important roles. Large <b>public sector companies</b> exist alongside private players. In reality, <b>almost all economies are mixed</b>. <b>Examples: India (post-1991), China (post-1978), Germany, Sweden.</b></li>
   366→  </ul>

   501→
   502→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q1.</span> What is economics? Explain its origin and what it deals with.</div>
   503→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   504→  <ul class="ans">
   505→    <li>Economics is the discipline that deals with how <b>choices are made by optimising limited resources</b> to satisfy needs and wants.</li>
   506→    <li>The word comes from the Greek <b>oikonomia</b> — <b>oikos</b> (household) + <b>nemein</b> (management) — meaning 'household management'.</li>
   507→    <li>Just as families must plan with limited income, <b>nations</b> must plan how to use resources efficiently.</li>
   508→    <li>It studies how economic entities — <b>consumers, producers, governments, financial institutions</b> — interact in an economy.</li>
   509→    <li>It explains wages, distribution of wealth, price determination, and the effect of policies and trade on markets and employment.</li>
   510→    <li>Good economic decisions are based on <b>data and analysis</b>, not guesswork.</li>
   511→  </ul></div>
   512→
   513→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q2.</span> Distinguish between needs and wants. Why can all wants not be satisfied?</div>
   514→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   515→  <ul class="ans">
   516→    <li><b>Needs</b> are essentials for survival — food, water, shelter.</li>
   517→    <li><b>Wants</b> are desires beyond essentials — gadgets, vacations, luxury items.</li>
   518→    <li>Wants are <b>unlimited and keep changing</b> — a person may upgrade from bicycle → motorbike → car.</li>
   519→    <li>All wants cannot be satisfied because <b>resources are limited</b> in quantity.</li>
   520→    <li>Hence individuals, enterprises and governments must constantly <b>choose</b> — and every choice sacrifices an alternative.</li>
   521→  </ul></div>
   522→
   523→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q3.</span> What is scarcity? Why is it called the central problem of economics?</div>
   524→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   525→  <ul class="ans">
   526→    <li>Scarcity is the condition where <b>resources are limited</b> while <b>human wants are unlimited</b>.</li>
   527→    <li>Both natural resources (water, coal) and human-made resources (capital, technology) are limited in quantity.</li>
   528→    <li>Resources also have <b>competing/alternative uses</b> — e.g., steel can make medical equipment, refrigerators or aircraft.</li>
   529→    <li>Because of scarcity, every economy must answer <b>what, how and for whom to produce</b>.</li>
   530→    <li>Thus scarcity forces <b>choice</b>, and every choice creates an <b>opportunity cost</b> — making it the central problem of economics.</li>
   531→  </ul></div>
   532→
   533→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q4.</span> Explain the concept of opportunity cost with the farmer's example.</div>
   534→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   535→  <ul class="ans">
   536→    <li>Opportunity cost is the <b>value of what is given up</b> when one alternative is chosen over others.</li>
   537→    <li>A farmer with a piece of land can grow either <b>barley or wheat</b>.</li>
   538→    <li>With limited land, water and labour, he must decide <b>how much of each crop</b> to produce.</li>
   539→    <li>When he moves from combination A to E (growing more barley), <b>some wheat must be sacrificed</b> — this sacrifice is the opportunity cost of growing barley.</li>
   540→    <li>Opportunity cost applies to all decision-makers — households (pocket money), enterprises, and governments (highways vs hospitals).</li>
   541→  </ul></div>
   542→
   543→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q5.</span> What is the Production Possibility Curve? What do the points on the curve show?</div>
   544→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   545→  <ul class="ans">
   546→    <li>The PPC is the curve showing <b>different combinations of two goods</b> that can be produced using <b>all available resources</b>.</li>
   547→    <li>It shows the <b>trade-off</b> between the two goods — e.g., barley and wheat.</li>
   548→    <li>It is <b>downward sloping</b>: producing more of one good requires sacrificing the other.</li>
   549→    <li>All points <b>on the PPC</b> show the <b>maximum output</b> achievable through <b>efficient use of resources</b>, avoiding wastage.</li>
   550→    <li>It helps enterprises and governments in <b>better planning and decision-making</b>.</li>
   551→  </ul></div>
   552→
   553→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q6.</span> Explain the three key questions that every economy must answer, with examples.</div>
   554→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   555→  <ul class="ans">
   556→    <li><b>What to produce?</b> — which goods and services, and in what quantities; e.g., water-intensive sugarcane/paddy vs drought-resistant millets/pulses; choosing sugarcane means giving up the gains of saved water and soil health (opportunity cost).</li>
   557→    <li><b>For whom to produce?</b> — which group of consumers to serve; e.g., school shoes (simple, durable, affordable), office shoes (formal, leather), sports shoes (grip, lightweight) — produced for different needs and purchasing power.</li>
   558→    <li><b>How to produce?</b> — choice of technique: <b>labour-intensive</b> (agriculture, handicrafts) or <b>capital-intensive</b> (steel, automobiles); depends on cost of capital, technology, product type, labour availability and government regulations.</li>
   559→    <li>All three arise from <b>scarcity</b> — limited resources must be allocated among unlimited wants.</li>
   560→  </ul></div>
   561→
   562→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q7.</span> Compare the three types of economic systems.</div>
   563→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   564→  <ul class="ans">
   565→    <li><b>Planned economy:</b> a central planning authority of the government makes all major decisions — what, how, for whom, and prices; government owns most resources; strict permits and licenses restrict competition and reduce motivation to innovate. Examples: former Soviet Union, North Korea, Cuba.</li>
   566→    <li><b>Market economy:</b> decisions are driven by <b>demand and supply</b> with little government intervention; the government acts like a <b>referee</b> ensuring safety and law &amp; order; private ownership dominates; competition brings better quality, lower prices and innovation. Examples: USA, Japan, Hong Kong.</li>
   567→    <li><b>Mixed economy:</b> combines both — private players and the government coexist and compete; large public sector companies operate alongside private enterprises; private players are regulated. Examples: India (post-1991), China (post-1978), Germany, Sweden.</li>
   568→    <li>In reality, <b>almost all economies are mixed</b> — pure systems rarely exist.</li>
   569→  </ul></div>
   570→
   571→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q8.</span> Why do pure market or pure planned economies rarely exist? Why is a mixed economy considered more practical?</div>
   572→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   573→  <ul class="ans">
   574→    <li>A pure <b>planned</b> system restricts competition — few enterprises, less innovation, and the government may fail to match production with people's needs.</li>
   575→    <li>A pure <b>market</b> system can produce unfair outcomes — markets allocate goods by ability to pay, so essentials may become unaffordable for vulnerable groups.</li>
   576→    <li>A <b>mixed economy</b> combines strengths — private enterprise brings competition, innovation and efficiency; government brings welfare programmes, public goods, fair competition rules and consumer protection.</li>
   577→    <li>It allows flexibility — the degree of government involvement can adjust to the country's needs (e.g., India's shift after 1991).</li>
   578→    <li>Hence almost all real economies — even the USA and Singapore — are mixed in practice.</li>
   579→  </ul></div>
   580→
   581→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q9.</span> Describe India's economic journey since Independence.</div>
   582→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   583→  <ul class="ans">
   584→    <li>After Independence, India followed a <b>state-led approach</b> similar to a planned economy.</li>
   585→    <li>The government controlled industries, allocated resources and regulated production through <b>licenses and permits</b>.</li>
   586→    <li>Key sectors — <b>banking, transport, heavy industries</b> — were dominated by the public sector.</li>
   587→    <li>By <b>1991</b>, the country faced serious economic difficulties.</li>
   588→    <li>The government introduced major <b>reforms</b>: reduced excessive regulations, encouraged private enterprise, opened the economy to global trade and investment, and increased competition.</li>
   589→    <li>Result: India gradually became a <b>market-oriented mixed economy</b> with the government still playing an important role.</li>
   590→  </ul></div>

   685→    <li><b>Future price expectations:</b> expect prices to fall → postpone purchases (demand ↓ now); expect rise → buy immediately (demand ↑ now) — e.g., waiting for Diwali/New Year discounts.</li>

   709→  <div class="plabel">✍️ Exam Points</div>
   710→  <ul class="pts">
   711→    <li><b>Price of related goods:</b> supply of one good depends on profitability of alternatives — wheat prices low but chickpea prices high → farmer grows more chickpeas next season.</li>
   712→    <li><b>Number of sellers:</b> more sellers → higher competition &amp; production → market supply exceeds demand → prices fall. Fewer sellers → lower supply → prices rise.</li>
   713→    <li><b>Technology:</b> improvement reduces cost of production → producers make &amp; supply more — e.g., drip irrigation, weather sensors raise crop output; cold storage lets mangoes reach distant markets, increasing supply.</li>
   714→    <li><b>Future expectations:</b> if producers expect higher demand/prices ahead, they produce more (supply ↑); expecting lower demand → cut production; e.g., potato wholesalers hold back stock to sell later at higher prices.</li>

   719→<div class="topic">
   720→  <div class="topic-h"><span class="tnum">T7</span><span class="tt">Market Equilibrium — The Perfect Balance ⚖️</span><span class="tick">☐ revised</span></div>
   721→  <div class="define"><div class="dl">📖 Definition</div><div class="dd"><b>Market equilibrium</b> — the point where <b>supply equals demand</b>; there is no excess supply (surplus) or excess demand (shortage), and prices remain stable unless external factors change.</div></div>
   722→  <div class="hing"><div class="ht">💡 Samajh (Hinglish)</div><div class="hb">Wo price jahan <b>buyers jitna khareedna chahte hain = sellers jitna bechna chahte hain</b>. Book example: <b>₹100 pe demand 12 kg = supply 12 kg</b> → EQUILIBRIUM! ✨ Is point pe market "cleared" ho jaata hai — na shortage, na surplus, aur price pe change ka koi pressure nahi. Graph me ye point <b>E</b> hai jahan demand curve supply curve ko kaat-ti hai.</div></div>
   723→  <div class="plabel">✍️ Exam Points</div>
   724→  <ul class="pts">
   725→    <li>Prices are determined by the <b>interaction of demand and supply</b> — negotiation between what buyers will pay and sellers will accept.</li>
   726→    <li><b>Equilibrium price = ₹100, equilibrium quantity = 12 kg</b> (chapter example) — where Qd = Qs.</li>
   727→    <li><b>At a lower price (₹40):</b> Qd (38 kg) &gt; Qs (6 kg) → <b>excess demand</b> (shortage) → pressure for price to rise.</li>
   728→    <li><b>At a higher price (₹150):</b> Qs (43 kg) &gt; Qd (8 kg) → <b>excess supply</b> (surplus) → pressure for price to fall.</li>
   729→    <li>At equilibrium the market is <b>'cleared'</b> — no shortage, no surplus, no pressure to change.</li>
   730→  </ul>

   758→  <div class="define"><div class="dl">📖 Definitions</div><div class="dd"><b>Price ceiling</b> — an imposed price control setting the <b>maximum</b> price a seller can charge. &nbsp;|&nbsp; <b>Price floor</b> — an imposed limit on how <b>low</b> a price can go; to be effective it must be set <b>above the equilibrium price</b>. &nbsp;|&nbsp; <b>Monopoly</b> — a market with a <b>single seller</b> controlling the entire supply of a unique product, with no close substitutes.</div></div>
   759→  <div class="hing"><div class="ht">💡 Samajh (Hinglish)</div><div class="hb">Market hamesha fair nahi hota — dawai ₹5000 ho jaye toh? 🤬 Tab sarkar referee banti hai: <b>ceiling</b> = chhat (upar ki limit — zyada mehnga nahi bech sakte, jaise sanitiser ₹100/200ml), <b>floor</b> = zameen (neeche ki limit — jaise minimum wage, taaki mazdoor ko loota na jaye).    762→    <li>Markets allocate goods by <b>willingness &amp; ability to pay</b> — essentials can become unaffordable, so fairness &amp; equity are needed for <b>vulnerable, low-income groups</b>.</li>
   763→    <li><b>Price ceiling:</b> maximum prices for essential goods like medicines — prevents overcharging.</li>
   764→    <li><b>Price floor:</b> e.g., <b>minimum wage</b> — ensures workers earn enough; effective only <b>above equilibrium price</b>.</li>
   765→    <li><b>Monopoly</b> harms consumers — higher prices, poorer quality, restricted supply; government keeps price &amp; quantity in check.</li>
   766→    <li><b>Regulators</b> ensure transparency: <b>RBI</b> (banking), <b>CCPA</b> (consumer rights &amp; unfair trade), <b>TRAI</b> (telecom), <b>SEBI</b> (securities market).</li>

   768→    <li><b>Hoarding</b> — accumulating goods beyond immediate need (fear of shortage / expected price rise); <b>Black marketing</b> — illegal trade of banned or regulated goods.</li>

   783→  <div class="plabel">✍️ Exam Points — limitations of government intervention</div>
   784→  <ul class="pts">

   788→  </ul>

   871→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q1.</span> What is demand? How is it different from mere desire?</div>
   872→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   873→  <ul class="ans">
   874→    <li>Demand is the quantity of a product that people are <b>willing and able to buy at a particular price</b>.</li>
   875→    <li>It depends on the buyer's needs, preferences, season, trend and income.</li>
   876→    <li>Desire alone is <b>not</b> demand — it must be supported by <b>purchasing power</b> (ability to pay).</li>
   877→    <li>Example: many may desire a luxury car, but only those who can pay create demand for it.</li>
   878→  </ul></div>
   879→
   880→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q2.</span> State and explain the law of demand with the mango example.</div>
   881→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   882→  <ul class="ans">
   883→    <li>The law of demand states that price and quantity demanded move in <b>opposite directions</b> (inverse relationship), other factors remaining constant.</li>
   884→    <li>When price rises, quantity demanded falls; when price falls, quantity demanded rises.</li>
   885→    <li><b>Mango example:</b> at ₹150/kg Srivalli bought 1 kg; at ₹100/kg she bought 2 kg; at ₹50/kg she bought 3 kg.</li>
   886→    <li>Plotting these gives a <b>downward-sloping demand curve</b> (DD').</li>
   887→    <li>One reason behind it is <b>diminishing marginal utility</b> — each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so willingness to pay falls.</li>
   888→  </ul></div>
   889→
   890→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q3.</span> Explain individual demand and market demand with a schedule.</div>
   891→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   892→  <ul class="ans">
   893→    <li><b>Individual demand</b> — the quantity one consumer wants to buy at different prices, other factors constant; its table form is the individual <b>demand schedule</b>.</li>
   894→    <li><b>Market demand</b> — the sum of all individual demands at each price (Q1 + Q2 + Q3).</li>
   895→    <li>Example: at ₹50 — Srivalli 3 kg + Alex 6 kg + Israt 9 kg = <b>market demand 18 kg</b>; at ₹100 it is 12 kg; at ₹150 it is 6 kg.</li>
   896→    <li>Plotting market demand gives the <b>market demand curve</b> (DmDm'), which is <b>flatter</b> than an individual curve.</li>
   897→    <li>Reason: many consumers respond together, so the same price change creates a <b>larger total quantity response</b> (₹150→₹50: individual +2 kg vs market +12 kg).</li>
   898→  </ul></div>

   900→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q4.</span> Explain the factors (other than price) that influence demand.</div>
   901→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   902→  <ul class="ans">
   903→    <li><b>Price of related goods:</b> substitutes (tea–coffee) — price of one ↑ → demand for other ↑; complements (car–petrol, printer–cartridge) — price of one ↑ → demand for other ↓.</li>
   904→    <li><b>Income:</b> higher household income → consumers buy more or better quality → demand rises.</li>
   905→    <li><b>Taste &amp; preference:</b> personal likes — Srivalli prefers mangoes even if oranges are cheaper.</li>
   906→    <li><b>Population size &amp; composition:</b> India's huge population boosts demand; more children → sports shoes; working adults → formal shoes; elderly → orthopaedic shoes.</li>
   907→    <li><b>Seasonality:</b> weather, festivals and habits — books in the new session, sweets in festivals, sweaters in winter.</li>
   908→    <li><b>Future price expectations:</b> expected fall → postpone buying; expected rise → buy now (pre-Diwali discounts).</li>

   911→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q5.</span> Distinguish between substitute goods and complementary goods, with examples.</div>
   912→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   913→  <ul class="ans">
   914→    <li><b>Substitutes</b> can replace each other in use — tea and coffee, mangoes and bananas.</li>
   915→    <li>When the price of a substitute rises, people switch to the cheaper one, so <b>demand for the related good increases</b>.</li>
   916→    <li><b>Complements</b> are used together to give utility — car and petrol, printer and cartridges, movie ticket and popcorn.</li>
   917→    <li>When the price of one complement rises, demand for the other <b>falls</b> — costlier movie tickets reduce popcorn demand.</li>
   918→    <li>Both show that demand for a good is <b>interconnected</b> with prices of related goods.</li>
   919→  </ul></div>

   921→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q6.</span> What is supply? Explain the law of supply.</div>
   922→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   923→  <ul class="ans">
   924→    <li>Supply is the quantity of a product that <b>sellers are willing and able to offer</b> at a particular price.</li>
   925→    <li><b>Law of supply (direct relationship):</b> as price rises, quantity supplied rises; as price falls, quantity supplied falls.</li>
   926→    <li>Reason: higher prices <b>increase profitability</b>, incentivising producers to raise output and attracting new firms.</li>
   927→    <li>Example: seller A supplies 1 kg at ₹50, 2 kg at ₹100, 3 kg at ₹150 per kg of mangoes.</li>
   928→    <li>The supply curve therefore slopes <b>upward</b>; market supply = sum of all individual supplies (A+B+C).</li>
   929→  </ul></div>

   931→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q7.</span> What is market equilibrium? Explain with the chapter's table example.</div>
   932→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   933→  <ul class="ans">
   934→    <li>Market equilibrium is the point where <b>quantity demanded equals quantity supplied</b>.</li>
   935→    <li>Here there is no pressure for prices to change and the market is <b>'cleared'</b> — neither shortage nor surplus.</li>
   936→    <li><b>Example:</b> at ₹100, quantity demanded = quantity supplied = <b>12 kg</b> → equilibrium price ₹100, equilibrium quantity 12 kg (point E where the curves intersect).</li>
   937→    <li><b>Below it (₹40):</b> Qd 38 kg &gt; Qs 6 kg → <b>excess demand</b> — shortage pushes prices up.</li>
   938→    <li><b>Above it (₹150):</b> Qs 43 kg &gt; Qd 8 kg → <b>excess supply</b> — surplus pushes prices down.</li>
   939→  </ul></div>
   940→
   941→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q8.</span> Does market equilibrium really exist in the real world? Explain with examples.</div>
   942→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   943→  <ul class="ans">
   944→    <li>In theory, equilibrium is a fixed intersection of demand and supply — but real markets are <b>dynamic</b>.</li>
   945→    <li>Technology, wages, interest rates, wars, political events, pandemics, weather and disasters keep changing demand and supply.</li>
   946→    <li>So equilibrium is <b>never stable</b> — markets keep adjusting towards a <b>new equilibrium</b>.</li>
   947→    <li><b>COVID-19 masks:</b> demand surged → supply lagged → prices rose → suppliers adjusted → prices fell → pandemic ended → prices returned to normal.</li>
   948→    <li><b>Hotel tariffs:</b> a Goa hotel charges ₹1,500 (off-season weekday), ₹8,000 (December weekend) and ₹25,000 (New Year's Eve), cutting tariffs by 40% overnight when a group cancels — prices move constantly with demand.</li>
   949→  </ul></div>

   951→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q9.</span> Why and how does the government intervene in the market?</div>
   952→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   953→  <ul class="ans">
   954→    <li><b>Why:</b> markets allocate goods by ability to pay — essentials may become unaffordable; fairness and equity are needed to protect <b>vulnerable, low-income groups</b>.</li>
   955→    <li><b>Price ceiling:</b> maximum price for essential goods (medicines) to prevent overcharging; e.g., sanitisers capped at ₹100/200 ml under the <b>Essential Commodities Act, 1955</b> during COVID-19.</li>
   956→    <li><b>Price floor:</b> minimum limit such as <b>minimum wage</b>, so workers are not exploited; effective when set above the equilibrium price.</li>
   957→    <li><b>Monopoly control:</b> a single seller can charge higher prices and restrict supply — the government keeps prices and quantity in check.</li>
   958→    <li><b>Regulators:</b> RBI (banking), CCPA (consumer protection), TRAI (telecom), SEBI (securities) ensure transparency and stop unfair practices like <b>hoarding</b> and <b>black marketing</b>.</li>
   959→  </ul></div>

   961→<div class="qa"><div class="qq"><span class="qn">Q10.</span> What are public goods? Why must the government provide them, and what are the limits of intervention?</div>
   962→  <div class="alab">Answer</div>
   963→  <ul class="ans">
   964→    <li><b>Public goods</b> are goods and services provided by the government for the benefit of all — roads, bridges, parks, street lighting, defence, sanitation.</li>
   965→    <li>Private companies do not provide them because they <b>do not generate direct profit</b>.</li>
   966→    <li><b>Park example (free-rider problem):</b> if every family thinks "others will pay, I'll use it free", the park never gets built — so collective goods need government funding.</li>
   967→    <li><b>Limits of intervention:</b> price controls below market levels reduce producers' motivation (wheat at ₹20 vs market ₹30 → shortages).</li>
   968→    <li>Heavy <b>compliance burdens</b> (multiple licences for a small restaurant) hurt the ease of doing business.</li>
   969→    <li>Excessive regulation <b>discourages innovation</b> — farmers won't invest in better seeds without adequate returns.</li>
   970→  </ul></div>
