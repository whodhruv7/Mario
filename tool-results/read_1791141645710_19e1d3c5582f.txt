   440→   440→Separation of Powers
   441→   441→The separation of powers among the legislature, executive, and 
   442→   442→judiciary—each with distinct responsibilities—enables democratic 
   443→   443→institutions to function effectively and responsibly. The legislature 
   444→   444→makes laws, the executive implements them, and the judiciary 
   445→   445→interprets them, thereby preventing the concentration of power in 
   446→   446→any one organ. This separation is essential for maintaining checks and 
   447→   447→balances among the three organs of the government. For example, 
   448→   448→while parliament has the power to amend the Constitution, the 
   449→   449→judiciary can review such amendments to ensure they are consistent 
   450→   450→with the spirit of the Constitution. Similarly, the executive power of 
   451→   451→enforcement agencies is regulated by laws and rules framed by the 
   452→   452→Parliament and can also be reviewed by competent courts. If a law is 
   453→   453→inconsistent with the constitutional provisions, the judiciary has the 
   454→   454→power to declare it unconstitutional.
   455→   455→The judiciary upholds the Constitution and plays a vital role in 
   456→   456→protecting and promoting democratic values and the rights of all 
   457→   457→sections of the society. It ensures the laws enacted by the Parliament 
   458→   458→of India and the State Legislative Assemblies are in accordance with 
   459→   459→the Constitution. The judiciary is an impartial and independent 
   460→   460→institution that safeguards citizens’ rights and upholds the spirit 
   461→   461→of the Constitution. Important initiatives, such as Public Interest 
   462→   462→Litigation (PIL), have also been taken by the judiciary from time to 
   463→   463→time to ensure access to justice for all.
