📝Big Questions — Exam-Ready Answers (points me)
Q1. What is demand? How is it different from mere desire?
Answer
  • Demand is the quantity of a product that people are willing and able to buy at a particular price.
  • It depends on the buyer's needs, preferences, season, trend and income.
  • Desire alone is not demand — it must be supported by purchasing power (ability to pay).
  • Example: many may desire a luxury car, but only those who can pay create demand for it.
Q2. State and explain the law of demand with the mango example.
Answer
  • The law of demand states that price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions (inverse relationship), other factors remaining constant.
  • When price rises, quantity demanded falls; when price falls, quantity demanded rises.
  • Mango example: at ₹150/kg Srivalli bought 1 kg; at ₹100/kg she bought 2 kg; at ₹50/kg she bought 3 kg.
  • Plotting these gives a downward-sloping demand curve (DD').
  • One reason behind it is diminishing marginal utility — each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so willingness to pay falls.
Q3. Explain individual demand and market demand with a schedule.
Answer
  • Individual demand — the quantity one consumer wants to buy at different prices, other factors constant; its table form is the individual demand schedule.
  • Market demand — the sum of all individual demands at each price (Q1 + Q2 + Q3).
  • Example: at ₹50 — Srivalli 3 kg + Alex 6 kg + Israt 9 kg = market demand 18 kg; at ₹100 it is 12 kg; at ₹150 it is 6 kg.
  • Plotting market demand gives the market demand curve (DmDm'), which is flatter than an individual curve.
  • Reason: many consumers respond together, so the same price change creates a larger total quantity response (₹150→₹50: individual +2 kg vs market +12 kg).
Q4. Explain the factors (other than price) that influence demand.
Answer
  • Price of related goods: substitutes (tea–coffee) — price of one ↑ → demand for other ↑; complements (car–petrol, printer–cartridge) — price of one ↑ → demand for other ↓.
  • Income: higher household income → consumers buy more or better quality → demand rises.
  • Taste & preference: personal likes — Srivalli prefers mangoes even if oranges are cheaper.
  • Population size & composition: India's huge population boosts demand; more children → sports shoes; working adults → formal shoes; elderly → orthopaedic shoes.
  • Seasonality: weather, festivals and habits — books in the new session, sweets in festivals, sweaters in winter.
  • Future price expectations: expected fall → postpone buying; expected rise → buy now (pre-Diwali discounts).
Q5. Distinguish between substitute goods and complementary goods, with examples.
Answer
  • Substitutes can replace each other in use — tea and coffee, mangoes and bananas.
  • When the price of a substitute rises, people switch to the cheaper one, so demand for the related good increases.
  • Complements are used together to give utility — car and petrol, printer and cartridges, movie ticket and popcorn.
  • When the price of one complement rises, demand for the other falls — costlier movie tickets reduce popcorn demand.
  • Both show that demand for a good is interconnected with prices of related goods.
Q6. What is supply? Explain the law of supply.
Answer
  • Supply is the quantity of a product that sellers are willing and able to offer at a particular price.
  • Law of supply (direct relationship): as price rises, quantity supplied rises; as price falls, quantity supplied falls.
  • Reason: higher prices increase profitability, incentivising producers to raise output and attracting new firms.
  • Example: seller A supplies 1 kg at ₹50, 2 kg at ₹100, 3 kg at ₹150 per kg of mangoes.
  • The supply curve therefore slopes upward; market supply = sum of all individual supplies (A+B+C).
Q7. What is market equilibrium? Explain with the chapter's table example.
Answer
  • Market equilibrium is the point where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
  • Here there is no pressure for prices to change and the market is 'cleared' — neither shortage nor surplus.
  • Example: at ₹100, quantity demanded = quantity supplied = 12 kg → equilibrium price ₹100, equilibrium quantity 12 kg (point E where the curves intersect).
  • Below it (₹40): Qd 38 kg > Qs 6 kg → excess demand — shortage pushes prices up.
  • Above it (₹150): Qs 43 kg > Qd 8 kg → excess supply — surplus pushes prices down.
Q8. Does market equilibrium really exist in the real world? Explain with examples.
Answer
  • In theory, equilibrium is a fixed intersection of demand and supply — but real markets are dynamic.
  • Technology, wages, interest rates, wars, political events, pandemics, weather and disasters keep changing demand and supply.
  • So equilibrium is never stable — markets keep adjusting towards a new equilibrium.
  • COVID-19 masks: demand surged → supply lagged → prices rose → suppliers adjusted → prices fell → pandemic ended → prices returned to normal.
  • Hotel tariffs: a Goa hotel charges ₹1,500 (off-season weekday), ₹8,000 (December weekend) and ₹25,000 (New Year's Eve), cutting tariffs by 40% overnight when a group cancels — prices move constantly with demand.
Q9. Why and how does the government intervene in the market?
Answer
  • Why: markets allocate goods by ability to pay — essentials may become unaffordable; fairness and equity are needed to protect vulnerable, low-income groups.
  • Price ceiling: maximum price for essential goods (medicines) to prevent overcharging; e.g., sanitisers capped at ₹100/200 ml under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 during COVID-19.
  • Price floor: minimum limit such as minimum wage, so workers are not exploited; effective when set above the equilibrium price.
  • Monopoly control: a single seller can charge higher prices and restrict supply — the government keeps prices and quantity in check.
  • Regulators: RBI (banking), CCPA (consumer protection), TRAI (telecom), SEBI (securities) ensure transparency and stop unfair practices like hoarding and black marketing.
Q10. What are public goods? Why must the government provide them, and what are the limits of intervention?
Answer
  • Public goods are goods and services provided by the government for the benefit of all — roads, bridges, parks, street lighting, defence, sanitation.
  • Private companies do not provide them because they do not generate direct profit.
  • Park example (free-rider problem): if every family thinks "others will pay, I'll use it free", the park never gets built — so collective goods need government funding.
  • Limits of intervention: price controls below market levels reduce producers' motivation (wheat at ₹20 vs market ₹30 → shortages).
  • Heavy compliance burdens (multiple licences for a small restaurant) hurt the ease of doing business.
  • Excessive regulation discourages innovation — farmers won't invest in better seeds without adequate returns.
