📝Big Questions — Exam-Ready Answers (Ch 6)
Q1. What is democracy? Explain its meaning and foundational principles.
Answer
  • Democracy is a form of government in which the source of power and authority lies with the citizens, who elect their representatives through free and fair elections.
  • It is founded on the principles of freedom, equality, justice, rights and duties.
  • Citizens have the right to vote, express their opinions, and participate in decision-making processes.
  • India is the world's largest democracy — population over 140 crore and a voter base of over 96.8 crore (2024).
  • The Constitution guarantees and protects the Fundamental Rights of all citizens without discrimination based on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth.
Q2. Trace the evolution of democratic traditions in India from early times.
Answer
  • The idea of democracy has been part of the Indian ethos since early times — the Vedic assemblies Sabha, Samiti and Vidhata involved collective decision-making.
  • The king was not an independent ruler but worked in consultation with assemblies, ministers and officers.
  • The Aikyamatya Sūktam of the Ṛig Veda (10.191.3) emphasises collective thinking, shared discussion and unity of purpose.
  • The Bauddha Saṁghas established by Gautama Buddha encouraged debate and discussion; members could choose their leader and decide through voting.
  • Invasions and British colonisation disrupted political structures, but the independence struggle revived democratic ideas.
  • The Constituent Assembly (1946) drafted the world's longest written Constitution in 2 years, 11 months and 18 days.
Q3. Explain popular sovereignty and the rule of law as principles of democracy.
Answer
  • Popular sovereignty: the ultimate source of power lies with the people; the state derives its authority from them.
  • A government elected through free and fair elections formulates policies and implements laws.
  • Every citizen 18 years and above has the right to vote through a secret ballot — the Universal Adult Franchise.
  • Rule of law: ensures equality before the law and equal protection of the law; no one is above the law.
  • Disputes are resolved in courts through procedures established by law — not by force or influence; no citizen can be punished without due process.
  • Together they promote justice, accountability and public trust in the democratic system.
Q4. List the six Fundamental Rights given in the Constitution with their Articles.
Answer
  • Right to Equality — Articles 14–18.
  • Right to Freedom — Articles 19–22.
  • Right Against Exploitation — Articles 23–24.
  • Right to Freedom of Religion — Articles 25–28.
  • Cultural and Educational Rights — Articles 29–30.
  • Right to Constitutional Remedies — Article 32.
  • These rights are enforceable in courts of law, indivisible and inalienable, subject to reasonable restrictions; remedies can be sought under Articles 32 and 226; the Right to Education (Article 21A, added 2009) gives free and compulsory education to children aged 6–14.
Q5. What is the separation of powers? How does it maintain checks and balances?
Answer
  • Separation of powers distributes responsibilities among the legislature, executive and judiciary so that each functions effectively and responsibly.
  • The legislature makes laws, the executive implements them, and the judiciary interprets them — preventing the concentration of power in any one organ.
  • Example: Parliament can amend the Constitution, but the judiciary can review amendments for consistency with the Constitution.
  • The executive's enforcement powers are regulated by laws framed by Parliament and can be reviewed by courts.
  • If a law is inconsistent with constitutional provisions, the judiciary can declare it unconstitutional.
  • The judiciary also upholds rights through initiatives like Public Interest Litigation (PIL).
Q6. How is the government kept accountable and transparent in a democracy? Explain with examples.
Answer
  • Democratic governments are accountable to citizens through elections, public debates, and engagement with civil society.
  • Question Hour — the first hour of a parliamentary session — lets members question the government and hold it accountable.
  • The Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005 allows citizens to seek information from government agencies — a major transparency law.
  • Accountability bodies include the CAG, Central Information Commission (CIC), Lokpal and Central Vigilance Commission (CVC).
  • Media and civil society act as the fourth pillar, raising public issues and enabling freedom of expression.
  • Decentralisation through rural and urban local bodies ensures people's participation at the grassroots level.
Q7. Describe the different types of democracy practised around the world, with examples.
Answer
  • Direct democracy — citizens directly participate in most decision-making; some representative features exist; difficult in large countries — e.g., Switzerland.
  • Representative (indirect) democracy — people elect representatives; periodic elections; government accountable — e.g., India.
  • Parliamentary democracy — executive members are part of the legislature; executive accountable to the legislature; people elect the legislature — e.g., India, Canada (UK adds a constitutional monarchy).
  • Presidential democracy — executive independent of the legislature; President elected by the people and accountable to them — e.g., USA.
  • Different societies institutionalise democracy in diverse ways, depending on citizen participation and how political power is structured.
Q8. What makes India a 'living democracy'? Explain with facts and figures.
Answer
  • India's democracy stands out for its scale, depth and inclusiveness — nearly a hundred crore voters form an unparalleled exercise in public participation.
  • In 2024, India had over 96.8 crore registered voters — more than the total population of many countries.
  • On average, one Member of Parliament represents about 25 lakh people.
  • Elections are conducted in 22 scheduled languages; election symbols allow even those who cannot read to vote independently.
  • Over one million polling stations are set up, including remote mountains, forests, deserts and islands — sometimes for a single voter.
  • Over 2,800 registered political parties reflect India's social and regional diversity, letting many viewpoints compete peacefully.
  • A three-tier system (Union, State, Local), ADCs and the PESA Act extend democracy to the grassroots.
Q9. Compare the Indian experience of women's right to vote with that of Britain and the USA. What reservation exists in local bodies?
Answer
  • In many countries, women had to launch long struggles: in Britain, full voting rights came only in 1928; in the USA, in 1920, after decades of protests.
  • In contrast, when the Constitution came into force in 1950, Indian women were granted Universal Adult Franchise without any prolonged legal battle.
  • However, the right to vote does not automatically ensure equal participation — social prejudices still limit women's representation.
  • Article 243(d): not less than one-third of seats in every Panchayat are reserved for women; 21 States + 2 UTs provide 50% reservation.
  • Article 243(t): not less than one-third in every Municipality; as of 2023, 17 States + 1 UT have 50% in Urban Local Bodies.
  • Case studies like South Manubankul (Tripura) — the 'women-friendly panchayat' — show women's participation in action.
Q10. What challenges does Indian democracy face? Describe the Emergency of 1975–77.
Answer
  • Challenges include illiteracy, misinformation and fake news (especially via social media), poverty, regionalism, gender inequality and social discrimination.
  • Gaps in the implementation of laws and policies reduce public trust; damaging public property and indifference also weaken democracy.
  • Emergency background: in the early 1970s, public dissatisfaction with the Indira Gandhi government grew due to rising unemployment, inflation and allegations of misgovernance.
  • In June 1975, a National Emergency was imposed on grounds of internal disturbance — Fundamental Rights were suspended, the press censored, and political leaders and activists arrested.
  • Mass movements led by Jayaprakash Narayan ('Lok Nayak') mobilised students and citizens, especially in Bihar and Gujarat.
  • The Emergency was lifted in 1977; elections followed and the ruling government was defeated — proving the strength of the ballot and the resilience of Indian democracy.
  • Articles 352, 356 and 360 deal with National Emergency, President's Rule and Financial Emergency respectively.
