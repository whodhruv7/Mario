📝Big Questions — Exam-Ready Answers (Ch 2)
Q1. Describe the layers of the Earth's interior.
Answer
  • The Earth is made up of three main layers: the crust, the mantle, and the core.
  • The crust is the outermost layer on which we live — 30–40 km thick under continents but only 5–7 km under the oceans.
  • The mantle lies below the crust — very thick, hot and mostly solid; the upper semi-molten part is the asthenosphere.
  • The core is the innermost and densest layer — the outer core is a fluid layer of iron and nickel; the inner core is a solid, hot, spinning metal ball.
  • The crust together with the upper mantle forms the lithosphere, which is broken into tectonic plates that move over the asthenosphere.
Q2. What is plate tectonics? What causes the movement of tectonic plates?
Answer
  • Plate tectonics is an important theory in earth science given by W.J. Morgan explaining the movement of the Earth's crust.
  • According to it, the outermost layer is not one single piece but is broken into several large and small pieces called tectonic plates (continental, oceanic, or mixed).
  • These plates move very slowly — usually a few centimetres per year — over the semi-molten layer beneath them.
  • The movement is caused by convection currents in the mantle: heat from the Earth's core makes molten material rise while cooler material sinks, and this continuous cycle pushes and pulls the plates.
  • Major plates include the Pacific, Eurasian, African, North American, South American, Indo-Australian and Antarctic plates.
Q3. Explain the three types of plate boundaries with their effects.
Answer
  • Convergent boundary — plates move towards each other: continental plates colliding form fold mountains like the Himalaya; an oceanic plate colliding with a continental plate sinks beneath it, leading to volcanic activity and earthquakes.
  • Divergent boundary — plates move away from each other: magma rises from below and forms new crust, creating features like mid-ocean ridges — e.g., the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
  • Transform boundary — plates slide past each other without creating or destroying crust, mainly causing earthquakes — e.g., the San Andreas Fault in the USA.
  • Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along these plate boundaries, especially around the Pacific Ocean, known as the Ring of Fire.
Q4. Distinguish between weathering and erosion. How does erosion affect human occupations?
Answer
  • Weathering is the breakdown of rocks into smaller pieces without any movement of the material — physical, chemical, or biological.
  • Erosion is the wearing away of soil and rocks that involves movement, by agents like water, wind, ice, or waves.
  • Effect on occupations: for farmers, erosion removes the fertile topsoil, lowering crop yields.
  • People near rivers and coasts lose land, houses and roads; in construction and mining, erosion destabilises land and poses safety risks.
  • Industries like tourism and fishing suffer when beaches, rivers and fertile lands are destroyed.
Q5. What are agents of gradation? Name the landforms created by each.
Answer
  • Agents of gradation are natural forces that wear down, transport and deposit materials, levelling the Earth's surface over time.
  • Running water — erodes rocks and soils to form valleys and plains (waterfalls, meanders, deltas).
  • Glaciers — scrape and carry material, carving U-shaped valleys, cirques, aretes, fjords, moraines.
  • Wind — shapes deserts: yardangs, ventifacts, deflation hollows, desert pavements, dunes.
  • Sea waves — erode coastlines into cliffs, caves, arches, stacks, and deposit beaches.
  • Groundwater — dissolves limestone creating Karst topography: caves, stalactites, stalagmites, sinkholes, underground rivers.
Q6. Explain how a river creates different landforms in its three courses.
Answer
  • Rivers shape the land through erosion, transportation and deposition along their course.
  • Upper course: steep gradients and strong erosive forces create V-shaped valleys, waterfalls and rapids — waterfalls form where hard rocks resist erosion while softer rocks below are worn away.
  • Middle course: the river begins to meander — eroding outer banks and depositing sediment on inner banks; forms oxbow lakes and floodplains.
  • Lower course: the slow river deposits large sediment loads, forming deltas, levees and alluvial fans at its mouth.
  • These landforms support agriculture (fertile banks), settlements, navigation and irrigation — e.g., the Grand Anicut (Kallanai) in Tamil Nadu.
Q7. How are coastal landforms created by waves and currents?
Answer
  • Waves and currents constantly move over the ocean surface and reshape the coastal zone.
  • Deposition: beaches are formed by sediments deposited by waves along the shoreline; they are popular tourist spots and act as natural barriers against strong waves.
  • Erosion: waves undercut the coast to form cliffs; flat wave-cut platforms are left behind as cliffs retreat.
  • Waves erode weak rock to form caves; caves on opposite sides of a headland meet to form arches; when arches collapse, isolated rock pillars called stacks remain.
  • These landforms influence tourism and may require coastal protection for settlements.
Q8. Describe the landforms made by glaciers.
Answer
  • Erosional landforms: U-shaped valleys (glaciers widen and deepen river valleys), cirques (bowl-shaped depressions at a glacier's head), aretes (sharp ridges between valleys), hanging valleys (where smaller glaciers meet larger ones) and fjords (deep, narrow inlets formed when the sea floods glacial valleys).
  • Depositional landforms — moraines: formed of till (rocks, soil, debris) left behind by melting glaciers.
  • Types: lateral (along the sides), terminal (at the end, marking the furthest advance) and medial (in the middle, when two glaciers' lateral moraines join).
  • Human importance: tourism (trekking, skiing, mountaineering), fjords as harbours and fishing bases, fertile glacial soil, and moraine-formed dams and lakes for water supply, irrigation and hydroelectric power.
  • Glaciers are also crucial sources of fresh water feeding rivers downstream.
Q9. Write a short note on the landforms created by underground water.
Answer
  • Underground water, especially in limestone or soluble rock areas, creates Karst topography through chemical weathering and erosion.
  • Caves — hollow spaces formed as acidic water dissolves rock.
  • Stalactites — icicle-shaped formations hanging from cave ceilings; stalagmites — formations rising from the floors; when both join, a pillar is formed.
  • Sinkholes (dolines) — depressions formed when the ground collapses into an underground cavity; underground rivers flow through cave systems.
  • These provide fresh water, tourism opportunities, and cultural or religious significance.
Q10. What causes landslides? How do human activities increase their risk?
Answer
  • Natural causes: heavy and continuous rainfall seeps into soil and rocks, increasing weight and reducing friction; earthquakes and volcanic eruptions shake and weaken slopes; steep slopes and loose, weathered rocks increase risk.
  • Human activities: deforestation, mining, road construction and unplanned construction on hillsides disturb the natural balance of slopes.
  • Poor drainage systems and improper land use allow excess water to accumulate, leading to sudden slope failure.
  • Avalanches, GLOFs and dust storms are other landform-related disasters — e.g., the Chamoli (Uttarakhand) flood of February 2021 damaged buildings, roads, bridges and hydel projects.
  • Mitigation needs awareness, planning and protecting slopes (as done in ancient India through contouring, bunding and terracing).
