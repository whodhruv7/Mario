💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Price ke alawa supply bhi kai cheezo pe depend karta hai: farmer soch raha hai wheat ya chana ugaye (jo mehnga, wahi boyega!); market me sellers zyada toh supply zyada; technology improve hui (drip irrigation 🌱, cold storage) toh production badh ke supply bhi; aur bhav badhne ki ummeed hai toh aaj bechna rok lo (aalu wale dhaniye kar lete hain!).
✍️ Exam Points
  • Price of related goods: supply of one good depends on profitability of alternatives — wheat prices low but chickpea prices high → farmer grows more chickpeas next season.
  • Number of sellers: more sellers → higher competition & production → market supply exceeds demand → prices fall. Fewer sellers → lower supply → prices rise.
  • Technology: improvement reduces cost of production → producers make & supply more — e.g., drip irrigation, weather sensors raise crop output; cold storage lets mangoes reach distant markets, increasing supply.
  • Future expectations: if producers expect higher demand/prices ahead, they produce more (supply ↑); expecting lower demand → cut production; e.g., potato wholesalers hold back stock to sell later at higher prices.
T7Market Equilibrium — The Perfect Balance ⚖️☐ revised
📖 Definition
Market equilibrium — the point where supply equals demand; there is no excess supply (surplus) or excess demand (shortage), and prices remain stable unless external factors change.
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Wo price jahan buyers jitna khareedna chahte hain = sellers jitna bechna chahte hain. Book example: ₹100 pe demand 12 kg = supply 12 kg → EQUILIBRIUM! ✨ Is point pe market "cleared" ho jaata hai — na shortage, na surplus, aur price pe change ka koi pressure nahi. Graph me ye point E hai jahan demand curve supply curve ko kaat-ti hai.
✍️ Exam Points
  • Prices are determined by the interaction of demand and supply — negotiation between what buyers will pay and sellers will accept.
  • Equilibrium price = ₹100, equilibrium quantity = 12 kg (chapter example) — where Qd = Qs.
  • At a lower price (₹40): Qd (38 kg) > Qs (6 kg) → excess demand (shortage) → pressure for price to rise.
  • At a higher price (₹150): Qs (43 kg) > Qd (8 kg) → excess supply (surplus) → pressure for price to fall.
  • At equilibrium the market is 'cleared' — no shortage, no surplus, no pressure to change.

T8Real World — Markets are Dynamic! 🌪️☐ revised
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Theory me equilibrium ek fixed point hai, but real world me sab chalta rehta hai — technology, wages, wars, elections, pandemic, mausam! 😵 Isliye market hamesha ek naye equilibrium ki taraf adjust karta rehta hai, kabhi settle nahi hota. COVID me masks 🏷️, aur Goa ke hotels — dono perfect examples.
✍️ Exam Points
  • In the real world, markets are dynamic — technology, wages, interest rates, wars, political events, pandemics, weather and disasters constantly change demand & supply.
  • So equilibrium is never stable — the market always keeps adjusting towards a new equilibrium.
  • COVID-19 masks (2020): demand surged rapidly → supply couldn't catch up → prices rose sharply → suppliers adjusted, prices fell → pandemic over → demand reduced → prices back to pre-pandemic levels.
  • Hotel tariffs (Goa, 100 rooms): off-season weekday (July Monday) ₹1,500 • tourist-season weekend (December Saturday) ₹8,000 • New Year's Eve ₹25,000 — and a 40% overnight cut if a group booking cancels, to fill empty rooms.
  • Tariff changes depend on: how fast rooms are booking, tariffs of nearby hotels, festivals/events, weather forecasts, days left before arrival, past booking trends.
T9Role of Government — Referee Mode 🏛️☐ revised
📖 Definitions
Price ceiling — an imposed price control setting the maximum price a seller can charge.  |  Price floor — an imposed limit on how low a price can go; to be effective it must be set above the equilibrium price.  |  Monopoly — a market with a single seller controlling the entire supply of a unique product, with no close substitutes.
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Market hamesha fair nahi hota — dawai ₹5000 ho jaye toh? 🤬 Tab sarkar referee banti hai: ceiling = chhat (upar ki limit — zyada mehnga nahi bech sakte, jaise sanitiser ₹100/200ml), floor = zameen (neeche ki limit — jaise minimum wage, taaki mazdoor ko loota na jaye). Ek akela seller poora market kaabu kare = monopoly — ye bhi sarkar check karti hai.
✍️ Exam Points — why & how government intervenes
  • Markets allocate goods by willingness & ability to pay — essentials can become unaffordable, so fairness & equity are needed for vulnerable, low-income groups.
  • Price ceiling: maximum prices for essential goods like medicines — prevents overcharging.
  • Price floor: e.g., minimum wage — ensures workers earn enough; effective only above equilibrium price.
  • Monopoly harms consumers — higher prices, poorer quality, restricted supply; government keeps price & quantity in check.
  • Regulators ensure transparency: RBI (banking), CCPA (consumer rights & unfair trade), TRAI (telecom), SEBI (securities market).
  • COVID sanitiser case: demand surged → hoarding & black marketing → government declared sanitisers essential under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, capping MRP at ₹100 for 200 ml; soon many companies started production and prices normalised.
  • Hoarding — accumulating goods beyond immediate need (fear of shortage / expected price rise); Black marketing — illegal trade of banned or regulated goods.
T10Public Goods + Limits of Intervention 🚧☐ revised
📖 Definition
Public goods — goods & services provided by the government for the benefit of all citizens — roads, bridges, public parks, street lighting, national defence, sanitation; their use by some does not prevent others from using them.
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Park wali story 🏞️: mohalle me park banwana hai, har family ₹5,000 de. Sab sochte hain — "dus log paise denge toh park ban jayega, main free me ghoom lunga!" 😜 Result: paise poore nahi aate, park kabhi nahi banta. Isi ko kehte hai free-rider problem — isliye aisi cheezein sarkar ko hi banana padti hai. But dhyan — zyada intervention bhi nuksaan karta hai (next points!).
✍️ Exam Points — public goods
  • Private companies don't provide them because they do not generate direct profit.
  • Examples: roads, bridges, parks, street lighting, national defence, sanitation & drainage systems.
  • Government provision ensures social welfare, economic development and equal access to essential services.
✍️ Exam Points — limitations of government intervention
  • Price distortions & reduced producer incentives: if govt fixes wheat at ₹20/kg when market price is ₹30 — farmers receive less → reduced production and shortages.
  • Compliance burdens: extensive regulations, licenses, permits — e.g., a small restaurant needs food safety, fire safety, pollution control and local clearances; time & cost discourage small entrepreneurs (hurts ease of doing business).
  • Discourages innovation & entrepreneurship: heavy regulation and price controls reduce incentives — farmers won't invest in better seeds or irrigation if returns are inadequate → long-term productivity falls.
🧠 Last-line for answers: A democratic government is accountable to the people — it must intervene to protect welfare, but carefully and in balance. ✔️
📖 Definition Bank — Chapter 9, Part 1 (likhne ke liye ready) 📖
Demand — quantity buyers are willing & able to purchase at a particular price.
Law of demand — price & quantity demanded move in opposite directions (inverse relation).
Individual demand — one consumer's purchase quantity at different prices.
Market demand — sum of all individual demands at each price.
Demand schedule — table of price vs quantity demanded; demand curve — its graph (DD', slopes downward).
Substitute goods — replace each other (tea–coffee); price of one ↑ → demand for other ↑.
Complementary goods — used together (car–petrol); price of one ↑ → demand for other ↓.
Related goods — products whose demand is interconnected.
Purchasing power — how much one unit of currency can buy at a particular time.
Diminishing marginal utility — extra satisfaction from each additional unit falls as consumption increases.
📖 Definition Bank — Chapter 9 (Part 2) 📖
Supply — quantity sellers are willing & able to offer at a particular price.
Law of supply — price & quantity supplied move together (direct relation); supply curve slopes upward.
Market supply — sum of all individual sellers' supplies (A+B+C = QS).
Market equilibrium — point where quantity demanded = quantity supplied; market is 'cleared'.
Equilibrium price/quantity — the price (₹100) & quantity (12 kg) at which Qd = Qs.
Price ceiling — government-set maximum price (e.g., sanitiser cap).
Price floor — government-set minimum price (e.g., minimum wage); effective above equilibrium.
Monopoly — single seller of a unique product, no close substitutes, high price-setting power.
Public goods — non-excludable goods provided by the government for all (roads, parks, defence).
Hoarding — stocking goods beyond need fearing shortages / expecting price rise.
Black marketing — illegal trade of banned or regulated goods & services.
Revenue — total money a business earns from sales before any expenses are deducted.
