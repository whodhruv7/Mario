History · Chapter 5
State and Society up to 1000 CE
Part AChapter Notes

The big picture. This chapter tracks how state and society grew in the Indian subcontinent up to 1000 CE. A society is a system of social relationships among people who share a territory, culture and a sense of belonging — it runs mainly on customs, not formal laws. A state is an organised political system based on rules and laws, with defined rights and duties for rulers and subjects. The broad evolution runs: kin-based clans (janas) → territorial states (janapadas) → 16 mahājanapadas → great empires (Mauryas, Guptas, Cholas) — while shared ideas like dharma and the ideal of the chakravarti samrāj treated the subcontinent as one political entity.

The Vedic period. The Vedas are the earliest known corpus of Indian literature — composed orally in the north-west (Sapta-Sindhu), with the Ṛig Veda (about 1,028 hymns) being the world’s oldest surviving poetic compositions. There are four Vedas: Ṛig Veda (hymns of praise), Yajur Veda (ritual instructions), Sāma Veda (hymns arranged for singing — foundation of the seven svaras of Indian music) and Atharva Veda (hymns for protection and healing); each has four parts — samhitā, brāhmaṇa, āraṇyaka, upaniṣhad. Society was organised as janas (clans); the rājā was mainly a clan chief who led in war, and three assemblies limited his power — the sabhā (small, elite, judicial), the samiti (larger, policy) and the vidhata (popular gathering of the jana). The name “Bharata” itself first appears in the Ṛig Veda, from the Bharata jana.

From janas to states. From about 1000 BCE, iron tools and the fertile Ganga plains created agricultural surplus — control of land, produce and trade routes became valuable, so more complex administration was needed. Identity shifted from kinship to territory: a janapada is literally “where a jana first set its feet.” By the 6th century BCE there were 16 mahājanapadas in two forms — monarchies (rājyas) and republics (gaṇa-saṁghas, like Vajji). Magadha (today’s Bihar) became the most powerful — strategic location, fertile plains, strong rulers — and later formed the base of the Mauryan Empire. Far south, the Sangam age (300 BCE–300 CE) saw the three crowned kingdoms of Tamilakam: Cholas (tiger), Cheras (bow), Pandyas (fish) — remember, these early kingdoms are centuries older than the imperial Cholas of the 9th–11th centuries CE.

Ideals of kingship. From the 6th century BCE rulers used titles like rājā, mahārāja and samrāj. The Yajur Veda’s coronation oath made a king promise to judge the strong and the weak impartially, protect the country and do good to the people; Kauṭilya’s Arthaśhāstra insisted that “only if a king is himself energetically active, do his officers follow him energetically.” Kingship was usually hereditary, yet references to kings being elected or expelled show royal authority was not always absolute. Monarchs also saw the whole subcontinent as one field of power: Jambudvīpa, Bhāratavarṣha, the aśhvamedha and rājasūya yajñas, and the chakravarti-kśhetra (“the area between Himavat and the sea”) — an ideal later echoed by Rajendra I’s title Gangaikonda.

How empires were administered. Kauṭilya’s Saptāṁga lists the seven limbs of a state — king, ministers, territory, forts, treasury, army and allies (“one wheel does not move the carriage”). The king consulted a council of ministers (mantri-pariṣhad), while kingdoms were layered: provinces → divisions → districts → groups of villages → villages (the smallest unit). Highlights: the Guptas (320–550 CE) created a new minister of peace and war (sāndhivigrahika), and their Damodarpur plates show a district office of five members; the Pallavas granted tax-free brahmadeya villages with active assemblies; the Chālukyas gave agrahārams to Brahmin settlements that became centres of learning; the Gurjara-Pratihāras fought the tri-partite struggle for Kannauj against the Pālas and Rāṣhṭrakūṭas; and the imperial Cholas ran maṇḍalam → valanāḍu → nāḍu → Ur, with self-reliant village assemblies that collected revenue, managed irrigation and settled disputes.

Village democracy — two case studies. The Uttaramerur inscription of the Chola king Parantaka I (10th century CE) records the Kudavolai (“ballot pot”) system: candidates’ names written on palm leaves went into a pot, and a young child drew the leaves in full public view to select members of the village assembly and its committees (variyams for tanks, justice and taxes) — candidates needed “honest earnings” and a pure mind. And one rock at Junagadh (Girnar, Gujarat) carries inscriptions of three rulers across ~700 years: Aśhoka’s Dhamma edicts, Rudradaman I’s Sanskrit record repairing the Sudarshana Lake, and Skandagupta’s restoration of the same lake.

Dharma and ethics. Dharma is not “religion” — it is duty, righteousness and moral conduct (“that which upholds beings”; root dhri), with dhamma as its Pali form used by Aśhoka. The Ṛig Veda’s ṛita is the cosmic order keeping nature and society in balance; samatva teaches that all beings share one essence. The Bhagavad Gītā stresses knowledge, faith, action and virtue. Ethics ran through governance too — Uttaramerur office-bearers had to show honest earnings.

Social structure — varṇa, jāti, family. The four varṇas — brāhmaṇas, kṣhatriyas, vaiśhyas, śhūdras — are first mentioned in the Puruṣhasūkta (Book 10, Ṛig Veda). Early on the order was occupational and flexible: Ṛig Veda 9.112.3 says “I am a poet, my father is a physician, my mother grinds corn,” and a Buddhist text holds that no one is a brāhmaṇa “by birth, only by deeds.” Jātis emerged later through intermarriage, endogamous migrant communities and regional differences — varṇas stayed four, jātis kept multiplying. Society was not fully rigid: the Mandsaur inscription records silk weavers who also took up archery and astrology. Family life ran kula → grāma → viśha → jana; gotra (patrilineal descent from a sage) regulated marriage; life moved through four āśhramas (brahmacharya, gṛihastha, vānaprastha, saṁnyāsa) and aimed at four puruṣhārthas (dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣha).

Women. In the Vedic period women took part in learning, rituals, chariot races and the sabhā — hymns are attributed to women sages like Apālā, Ghoṣhā and Lopāmudrā. The Manu-Smṛiti says “where women are honoured, there the gods rejoice.” Their position later declined with changing conditions, but women kept contributing: Prabhāvatī Gupta ruled as regent and issued land grants in her own name; Sangam poetesses like Avvaiyar became famous; the Chola queen Sembiyan Mahādevī endowed temples.

Religion and Bhakti. Vedic deities were linked to nature (sun, rain, fire, dawn) and worshipped through yajñas — a vision of nature as sacred that survives in Chhath and Makar Sankranti. From the mid-1st millennium BCE, renunciants (śhramaṇas, bhikṣhus, parivrājakas — including Gautama Buddha and Mahāvīra) questioned elaborate ritual. The tradition then evolved towards devotion to Viṣhṇu, Śhiva and Śhakti: Bhakti — a direct, personal bond with god without costly rituals, open to every class and gender. In the Tamil country the 12 Āḻvārs (Viṣhṇu) and 63 Nāyanmārs (Śhiva) created the great body of Tamil Bhakti literature from the 6th century.

Education and literature. Education was holistic, taught in the gurukula through a sacred guru–śhiṣhya bond — the Vedas plus grammar, logic, mathematics, medicine, astronomy, arts and archery. Great centres included Takshashila, Nalanda (427 CE), Valabhi (c. 480 CE) and Vikramashila, drawing students from abroad. Classics include Pāṇini’s grammar, Kālidāsa’s Raghuvaṁśha, Tiruvalluvar’s Tirukkural and Bāṇabhaṭṭa’s Kādambarī — celebrated as one of the world’s first novels.

Economy. The Mauryan state took a basic land tax of about one-sixth (1/6) of the produce and actively built irrigation — the Sudarshana Lake dam (built under Chandragupta Maurya, repaired by Rudradaman I, restored by Skandagupta) and Karikala Chola’s Grand Anicut, still in use today. From the 6th century BCE two great routes — Uttarāpatha (north) and Dakṣiṇāpatha (south) — linked inland regions to ports like Muziris and Poompuhar. Guilds (śhreṇīs) — 18 types in the Jātaka — fixed quality and prices, ran guild courts and worked as banks: the Nashik cave inscription records 3,000 kāhāpaṇas deposited with weavers’ guilds, whose interest maintained Buddhist monasteries.

Civics · Chapter 6
Democracy
Part AChapter Notes

What democracy means. Democracy is a form of government in which the source of power and authority lies with the citizens, who elect representatives through free and fair elections; it is founded on freedom, equality, justice, rights and duties. India is the world’s largest democracy — over 140 crore people and about 96.8 crore voters (2024). Its Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950, guaranteeing Fundamental Rights to every citizen without discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.

India’s long democratic story. Democratic ideas are not new to India: Vedic assemblies — the sabhā, samiti and vidhata — practised collective decision-making, some early states were republics (gaṇa-saṁghas), and the Buddha’s Saṁgha chose its leader and voted on decisions; a Ṛig Veda verse even praises assemblies whose “counsel is one.” British colonisation restricted people’s participation, but the freedom struggle revived democratic ideas. After independence, the Constituent Assembly (formed 1946) took 2 years, 11 months and 18 days to draft the world’s longest written Constitution, with Dr. B.R. Ambedkar chairing the Drafting Committee. Article 368 allows lawful amendments, keeping the Constitution a living, flexible document.

Seven non-negotiable principles. (1) Popular sovereignty — ultimate power lies with the people, exercised through Universal Adult Franchise: every citizen 18+ votes by secret ballot. (2) Rule of law — no one is above the law; disputes are settled by courts, not force. (3) Fundamental Rights — six enforceable rights safeguarding freedom and dignity. (4) Separation of powers — legislature, executive and judiciary check one another. (5) Accountability and transparency — through elections, Question Hour and the RTI Act (2005). (6) Multi-party system — a party or coalition with more than 50% of seats forms the government; the rest form the opposition. (7) Safeguarding vulnerable groups — Article 46 directs special care for weaker sections, especially SCs and STs.

Fundamental Rights. The six Fundamental Rights with their Articles: Equality (14–18), Freedom (19–22), against Exploitation (23–24), Freedom of Religion (25–28), Cultural & Educational (29–30), and Constitutional Remedies (32) — under which courts can be moved whenever a right is violated (also Article 226). They are enforceable, indivisible and inalienable. Article 21A (added 2009) makes education free and compulsory for children aged 6–14. Exam trap: don’t confuse Fundamental Rights (enforceable) with Fundamental Duties (our responsibilities) or Directive Principles (guidelines for the state).

Institutions, accountability, media. Power is shared across a three-tier system — Union, State and local governments. Independent bodies keep the government answerable: the CAG audits accounts, the CIC implements RTI, the Lokpal and CVC investigate wrongdoing; Question Hour lets members question the government daily. The media is called the fourth pillar of democracy — informing people and raising public issues. The RTI Act (2005) became law through a fully democratic journey: citizens’ concerns → parliamentary committees → expert review → debate → safeguards → law — proof that ordinary people can shape legislation.

Types of democracy. In a direct democracy citizens decide most matters themselves — realistic only in small countries like Switzerland. Large countries use representative democracy, as in India. Within that, a parliamentary system (India, UK, Canada) draws its executive from the legislature and keeps it accountable to it — you vote for your MP, not the PM. A presidential system (USA) elects the executive directly and separately from the legislature. There is no single model — but freedom, equality, participation, rule of law, rights and duties are common everywhere.

India’s living democracy. One MP represents about 25 lakh people; elections run in 22 scheduled languages, with symbols helping non-literate voters vote independently; there are over a million polling stations — sometimes for a single voter — and 2,800+ registered parties. Tribal regions get special self-governance: Autonomous District Councils in the north-east and the PESA Act (1996), which empowers the Gram Sabha in Scheduled Areas. Grassroots proof: Jethipura (Gujarat) won the national NDRGGSP award (2019) for its effective Gram Sabhas, and South Manubankul (Tripura) is a nationally recognised women-friendly panchayat.

Women and democracy. Indian women got full voting rights from the very start (1950) — while American women waited until 1920 and British women until 1928. But the right to vote is not the same as equal participation; social prejudice still limits women’s representation in politics. The Constitution pushes participation forward: not less than one-third of Panchayat seats are reserved for women (Article 243D) and one-third in Municipalities (Article 243T); 21 states and 2 UTs have raised the Panchayat share to 50%.

The Emergency (1975–77). Rising dissatisfaction, unemployment, inflation and protests against the government of Indira Gandhi led to a National Emergency in June 1975, declared on grounds of internal disturbance (Emergency provisions sit in Articles 352, 356 and 360). Most Fundamental Rights were suspended, the press was censored, and opposition leaders were arrested. Jayaprakash Narayan — “Lok Nayak” — mobilised students and citizens, especially in Bihar and Gujarat. The Emergency was lifted in 1977; elections followed and the ruling government was defeated — showing both democracy’s vulnerability and its resilience.

Challenges and young citizens. Indian democracy still faces illiteracy, poverty, inequality, regionalism, gender discrimination and misinformation on social media, which can manipulate public opinion. Everyone must act responsibly — not spreading rumours, not damaging public property, staying informed through newspapers and reliable news. Programmes like NSS, NCC and Bharat Scouts & Guides build civic responsibility among students. Democracy, finally, is not merely a system of government but a continuous process shaped by active, informed citizens.

Civics · Chapter 7
Elections
Part AChapter Notes

Why elections matter. An election is the process through which we exercise our right to vote and fulfil our responsibility as citizens; regular, periodic elections lie at the core of democracy. They deliver four things — accountability (the government must answer to the people), representation (our voice reaches government through a representative), legitimacy (a mandate from the people makes government lawful) and equality (every vote counts the same — a PM’s and a labourer’s alike). Our vote is the principal means of exercising this right.

Three ways to turn votes into seats. In First-Past-The-Post (FPTP), used for Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabha elections, the candidate with the most votes wins even with less than 50% — in the textbook’s Haritbhumi, Party A won 40%, B 35%, C 25%, and A formed the government. In the majority system, a winner needs over 50%; if nobody crosses it, the top two fight a second round — in Ratnadeep, Y won the second round with 55%. In Proportional Representation (PR), used for Rajya Sabha, Presidential and Vice-Presidential elections, seats go in proportion to votes — in Swarnalok (100 seats), a party with 40% of the votes gets 40 seats and power is shared.

The single transferable vote (STV). PR in India works through STV. Voters mark preferences (1st, 2nd, 3rd…); the quota — the minimum votes needed to win — equals total valid votes ÷ seats to be filled. Candidates reaching the quota on first preferences are elected; the lowest-polling candidate is eliminated and their votes transfer to the second preferences marked on those ballot papers; this repeats until enough candidates are elected. Six states are bicameral (they also have a Vidhan Parishad chosen this way): Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh — its members are elected by MLAs and local bodies, and by graduates and teachers, with some nominated by the Governor.

The laws. The Representation of the People Act, 1950 builds the foundation — allocation of seats, delimitation of constituencies and preparation of electoral rolls — and guarantees every citizen 18+ the right to vote without discrimination. The RPA, 1951 polices the battle — nomination of candidates, campaigns, voting procedures and post-election disputes — and bans corrupt practices: bribery, appeals to vote on religion, caste, race, community or language (communal appeal), and misuse of government personnel (gazetted officers, judges, armed forces, police). A separate Presidential & Vice-Presidential Act (1952) covers those elections. Memory hook: 1950 = rolls, 1951 = conduct and disputes.

Delimitation. Constituency boundaries are not fixed forever, because populations shift. Without delimitation one MP might represent 5 lakh people and another 25 lakh — defeating equal representation. The Constitution’s Article 82 mandates the Delimitation Commission, which fixes the number of seats and the boundaries so the seats-to-population ratio stays nearly equal. It has been set up four times: 1952, 1963, 1973 and 2002.

The Election Commission of India (ECI). The Constitution vests in the ECI the “superintendence, direction and control” of the entire election process (Articles 324–329). Established on 25 January 1950 — now celebrated as National Voters’ Day — the ECI is an autonomous, permanent, constitutional body that conducts elections to Parliament, state legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President; at state level it works through the Chief Electoral Officer and district officers. Its four big jobs: (1) creating and maintaining electoral rolls — enumerators visit every household, and the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) adds new 18-year-olds while removing dead, shifted, duplicate or untraceable entries; (2) fixing the schedule — around weather, the agricultural cycle, exams and festivals; (3) registering political parties and allotting symbols — it insists on inner-party elections and acts quasi-judicially in symbol disputes; (4) ensuring free, fair, inclusive elections — home voting for citizens above 85 and PwDs with 40% benchmark disability (first nationwide in 2024), Braille EVMs, and digital apps: Suvidha (candidate permissions), Voter Helpline, ENCORE (rolls & forms), Saksham (PwD services), cVIGIL (report MCC violations), Sugam (vehicles) and ETPBS (postal ballots for service voters).

Political parties. Voters learn about candidates, policies and vision mainly through parties, which set the election agenda with manifestos and campaigns, organise public opinion and give people real choices. India’s multi-party system reflects its diversity of languages, cultures, religions and regions. The ECI recognises parties as national or state parties by strict criteria — for example, 6% of valid votes in four or more states plus four Lok Sabha seats (or 2% of Lok Sabha seats from at least three states) for a national party; 6% of votes in a state election plus seats for a state party. Parties that don’t qualify remain registered-unrecognised (RUPPs).

Defection and coalitions. Defection — an elected member abandoning the party on whose ticket they won (the “Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram” syndrome) — destabilised governments, so the 52nd Constitutional Amendment (1985), the Anti-Defection Law, lets the Speaker or Chairman disqualify a member who voluntarily gives up party membership or votes against the party’s whip. Coalitions are the other face of a multi-party system: the alliance era began in 1967; the Janata Party formed the first national coalition government in 1977 after the Emergency; since the late 1990s the NDA (led by BJP) and UPA (led by INC — reconstituted in 2023 as the INDIA alliance) have been the major blocs.

Challenges — and one first vote. With 96.8 crore voters, challenges persist: misinformation and fake news, intimidation and money power. The ECI’s answers include the RPA, the Model Code of Conduct (MCC), VVPAT slips (so voters can verify their vote) and awareness campaigns. The chapter’s case study — Ishani’s first vote — shows inclusion in action: online registration, wheelchair access and volunteers for her disabled brother, home voting for her 89-year-old grandmother, acceptance of documents other than Voter ID, and postal ballots (ETPBS) for police and army on duty — though the wall writings and posters near polling day were actually MCC violations. No voter is left behind: this is India’s “festival of democracy.”

Part B10 Must-Know Definitions
TermExam-ready definition
ConstituencyA territorial area whose voters elect one representative to the legislature.
Electoral systemThe method of converting votes cast into seats in the legislature.
First-Past-The-Post (FPTP)The plurality system: the candidate with the most votes wins, even with less than 50% — used for Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabha elections.
Majority systemThe two-round system: a winner needs more than 50% of votes; otherwise the top two candidates compete in a second round.
Proportional Representation (PR)Seats are allotted to parties in proportion to the total votes they receive — used for Rajya Sabha, Presidential and Vice-Presidential elections.
Single Transferable Vote (STV)A PR method in which voters mark preferences; votes of eliminated candidates are transferred until enough candidates reach the quota.
QuotaThe minimum votes needed to win under STV = total valid votes ÷ seats to be filled.
DelimitationThe process of fixing the number of seats and constituency boundaries so the seats-to-population ratio stays nearly equal — mandated by Article 82.
Defection / Anti-Defection LawDefection: abandoning the party on whose ticket a member was elected. The 52nd Amendment (1985) disqualifies members who defect or defy the whip; the Speaker/Chairman decides.
VVPATVoter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail — a printed slip that lets voters verify that their vote went to the candidate they chose.
