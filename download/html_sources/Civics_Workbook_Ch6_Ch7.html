T1Democracy — Meaning + India 🇮🇳☐ revised
📖 Definition
Democracy — a form of government in which the source of power and authority lies with the citizens of the country, who elect their representatives through free and fair elections. It is founded on the principles of freedom, equality, justice, rights and duties.
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Simple seedha: janta boss, neta employee! Log apne representatives vote se chunte hain, wahi policies banate hain. India is the world's largest democracy — 140+ crore ki population, aur 96.8 crore voters (2024) — itna bada election kahin nahi hota! Backbone hai hamari Constitution: adopted 26 November 1949, in force 26 January 1950 (isliye Republic Day!).
✍️ Exam Points
  • Citizens have the right to vote, express opinions, and participate in decision-making.
  • The Constitution guarantees and protects Fundamental Rights of all citizens.
  • No citizen is discriminated against on the basis of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth.
  • It encourages citizen participation at the national, state, and local levels through elections.
  • Constitution of India: adopted 26 November 1949, came into force 26 January 1950.
T2Tracing Democratic Traditions — Ancient se Aaj Tak 🕰️☐ revised
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Log sochte hain democracy India me British se aayi — wrong! Iska seedha Vedic times me hai: Sabha, Samiti, Vidhata jaise assemblies me collective decisions hote the. Buddha ke Saṁghas me voting se leader chuna jaata tha! Phir invasions aur British rule ne sab disturb kiya, but azadi ki ladai ne democratic ideas wapas jagaya. Result: Constituent Assembly (1946) ne 2 saal 11 mahine 18 din me duniya ka longest written Constitution banaya — full dedication! 💪
🌳 System — India ke Democratic Traditions ka Safar (chronological)
  • Vedic period — assemblies: Sabha, Samiti, Vidhata (collective decision-making); king ruled in consultation, not independently
    • Ṛig Veda 10.191.3 (Aikyamatya Sūktam) — "their counsel is one, their assembly is one, their mind is one" — consultation, consensus, shared responsibility
  • Early republican states — the gaṇas / saṁghas
  • Bauddha Saṁghas — monastic community established by Gautama Buddha
    • Encouraged debate and discussion; members chose their leader and took decisions through voting
  • Invasions + British colonisation (19th century) — disrupted political structures, limited people's participation
  • Independence struggle — revived and strengthened democratic ideas among people
  • Constituent Assembly (formed 1946) — drafted the Constitution in 2 years, 11 months, 18 days
    • World's longest written Constitution; guided by the Constituent Assembly's Debates (CAD) and global democratic values
    • Dynamic document — evolves through amendments under Article 368 (robust, flexible, transformative, responsive)
✍️ Exam Points
  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar was the Chairman of the Drafting Committee of the Constitution.
  • Democratic ethos in India did not emerge suddenly — it evolved through a long historical process.
T3Principle 1 & 2 — Popular Sovereignty + Rule of Law 👑📜☐ revised
📖 Definitions
Popular sovereignty — the idea that the state derives its authority from the people; the ultimate source of power lies with them.  |  Universal Adult Franchise — the right of every citizen 18 years of age and above to vote through a secret ballot.  |  Rule of Law — the principle that no one is above the law; it ensures equality before the law (every individual, irrespective of status, treated equally) and equal protection of the law (all persons in similar circumstances treated equally, without discrimination).
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Do bodyguard concepts hain democracy ke: Popular sovereignty — "power public se aata hai" (government ki license janta renew karti hai har 5 saal me!). Rule of Law — "law sabke upar, sabki barabar" — minister ho ya aam aadmi, todne pe same court, same punishment. Koi bhi force ya paisa se faisla nahi kar sakta, sab procedure established by law se hoga — bina due process koi punish nahi ho sakta!
✍️ Exam Points
  • A government elected through free and fair elections formulates policies and implements laws.
  • Under rule of law, disputes are resolved in a court of law through procedures established by law — not by force or personal influence.
  • No citizen can be punished without due process; everyone gets a fair opportunity to defend their rights against arbitrary action.
  • The rule of law promotes justice, accountability, and trust in the democratic system.
T4Principle 3 — Fundamental Rights (6 FRs) 🛡️☐ revised
📖 Definition
Fundamental Rights — rights enshrined in the Indian Constitution that safeguard the rights, freedom, and dignity of citizens; they are enforceable in courts of law, indivisible and inalienable, subject to reasonable restrictions as per the procedure established by law.
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Ye 6 rights democracy ke shield hain — articles ke saath ratto, exam me seedha poocha jata hai! Trick: "E-F-E-R-C-C" — Equality, Freedom, Exploitation-not, Religion, Cultural, Constitutional-Remedies. Aur Article 32 ko Ambedkar ne "heart and soul" kaha — kyunki iske bina baaki rights enforce nahi ho sakte! Koi right todne pe Articles 32 & 226 (Supreme Court / High Court) me ja sakte ho. Bonus: RTE — Article 21A (2009) — 6 se 14 saal ke bachcho ke liye free & compulsory education.
🌳 System — 6 Fundamental Rights (Articles ke saath)
  • Right to Equality — Articles 14–18
  • Right to Freedom — Articles 19–22
  • Right Against Exploitation — Articles 23–24
  • Right to Freedom of Religion — Articles 25–28
  • Cultural and Educational Rights — Articles 29–30
  • Right to Constitutional Remedies — Article 32
    • Violations → legal action; remedies available under Articles 32 and 226
✍️ Exam Points
  • Right to Education (Article 21A) — added in 2009; free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14.
  • The Constitution also outlines Fundamental Duties and Directive Principles of State Policy.
T5Principle 4 — Separation of Powers ⚖️☐ revised
📖 Definition
Separation of powers — distribution of powers among the legislature, executive, and judiciary, each with distinct responsibilities, preventing the concentration of power in any one organ.  |  PIL (Public Interest Litigation) — a legal action initiated in a court of law for the enforcement of public interest.
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Ek hi organ ke paas sab power? Dictatorship ban jayegi! Isliye 3 organs ka division of labour: Legislature = law BANANE wala, Executive = law CHALANE wala, Judiciary = law SAMJHANE wala (interpret). Aur sab ek dusre pe nazar rakhte hain = checks and balances — jaise teen dost ek project me ek dusre ki galtiyan pakadte hain! Judiciary koi law unconstitutional declare kar sakti hai, aur PIL ke through common man bhi court ke darwaze khat-khata sakta hai.
🌳 System — 3 Organs + Checks & Balances
  • Legislature — makes laws (Parliament, State Assemblies)
    • Has power to amend the Constitution
  • Executive — implements laws
    • Enforcement agencies regulated by laws and rules framed by Parliament; can be reviewed by courts
  • Judiciary — interprets laws
    • Reviews amendments for consistency with the Constitution; can declare a law unconstitutional; impartial and independent; safeguards rights via PIL
✍️ Exam Points
  • This separation is essential for maintaining checks and balances among the three organs of the government.
  • The judiciary ensures laws enacted by Parliament and State Legislative Assemblies are in accordance with the Constitution.
T6Principle 5, 6, 7 — Accountability + Multi-Party + Vulnerable Groups 🔍☐ revised
📖 Definitions
Multi-party system — a system in which several political parties contest elections, representing diverse voices and interests; the party or coalition securing more than 50 per cent of the seats forms the government, the rest form the opposition.  |  Civil society — voluntary groups, NGOs and community associations that function interdependently within society.
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Accountability ka funda: government jawab-deh hai — elections, public debates, civil society sab judge ban ke baithte hain. Sabse bada tool = RTI Act 2005 — koi bhi citizen government departments se information maang sakta hai (transparency!). Multi-party system me har ideology ko apni party mil sakti hai — 2800+ registered parties India me! Aur history me weaker sections (SC/ST) ka exploitation hua hai, isliye Article 46 unke liye special care bolta hai.
✍️ Exam Points
  • Through elections, public debates, and engagement with civil society, citizens evaluate the government's actions — this builds trust.
  • RTI Act, 2005 — allows citizens to seek information from government agencies; enacted through democratic processes (reviewed by expert groups, debated in Parliament, safeguards for national security).
  • Political parties function within the framework of the Representation of the People Act, 1951.
  • Article 46 — the State shall promote with special care the educational and economic interests of weaker sections, especially Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and protect them from social injustice and exploitation.
  • Question Hour — first hour of a parliamentary session when members ask questions to the government, checking its work (accountability).

T8Types of Democracy — 4 Models 🌍☐ revised
📖 Definition
Democracy does not offer a single model of government — different societies institutionalise it in diverse ways, depending on how citizens participate in decision-making and how political power is structured.
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
School wala example yaad karo: Team A (sab students vote karte hain bade decisions pe — direct democracy lite), Team B (class rep chunta hai president — indirect/representative), Team C (students president + council dono chunte hain, president boss — presidential feel), Team D (selected candidates hi contest kar sakti hain, president ke paas saara power — democracy sirf naam ka!). Ye hi duniya ke 4 models hain. India = Parliamentary (PM + council of ministers, accountable to legislature), USA = Presidential, Switzerland = Direct!
🌳 System — Types of Democracy (iske under kya)
  • Direct Democracy — e.g., Switzerland
    • Citizens directly participate in most decision-making processes
    • Some features of representative democracy also exist
    • Difficult to follow in large countries
  • Representative (Indirect) Democracy — e.g., India
    • People elect representatives; they do not directly govern
    • Periodic elections; government accountable to the people
    • Two sub-models:
      • Parliamentary — e.g., India, Canada: executive members are part of the legislature; executive accountable to the legislature; people elect the legislature, not the executive
      • Presidential — e.g., USA: executive is independent of the legislature; President elected by the people, accountable to the people

✍️ Exam Points — 2 important definitions
  • Federalism — a system in which power and responsibilities are shared between the central and state governments, as expressed in the central, state and concurrent lists; residual power remains with the central government.
  • Sovereignty — from Latin superanus ('supreme'); the supreme, final, and independent legal authority of a State over its territory and citizens.
T9India's Living Democracy — Scale + Women + Grassroots 📊☐ revised
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
India ki democracy "living" kyun? Scale dekho: 96.8 crore voters, ek MP ~25 lakh logon ko represent karta hai, 22 scheduled languages me elections, 1 million+ polling stations — jungle, pahad, desert, island sab me (kahin kahin 1 voter ke liye bhi booth!). Women ke case me India ahead tha — Britain (1928) aur USA (1920) me women ne vote ke liye ladhna pada, but India ne 1950 me hi sabko Universal Adult Franchise de diya, bina ladai! Aur grassroots pe Panchayati Raj — villages me hi self-government (village = unit of grassroots democracy).
🌳 System — Facts that make India's democracy "living"
  • Scale: 96.8 crore+ registered voters (2024) — more people vote in India than the total population of many countries
  • Representation: on average one MP represents about 25 lakh people
  • Languages: elections conducted in 22 scheduled languages; election symbols let even those who cannot read vote independently
  • Reach: over one million polling stations (mountains, forests, deserts, islands) — sometimes for a single voter
  • Parties: over 2,800 registered political parties — social and regional diversity, many viewpoints compete peacefully
✍️ Exam Points — women & grassroots (Articles important!)
  • Women & the right to vote: Britain — full voting rights only in 1928; USA — 1920, after decades of protests. In India, the Constitution (in force 1950) extended Universal Adult Franchise to all citizens — men and women alike, without prolonged battles — though social prejudices still limit women's equal representation.
  • Article 243(d) — not less than one-third seats reserved for women in every Panchayat (by direct election); 21 States + 2 UTs have gone to 50% in Panchayati Raj Institutions.
  • Article 243(t) — not less than one-third for women in every Municipality; as of 2023, 17 States + 1 UT have 50% in Urban Local Bodies.
  • Three-tier system: Union, State and Local governments; Autonomous District Councils (ADCs) in north-eastern regions (legislative + judicial authority to preserve tribal customs); PESA Act, 1996 — Panchayats Extension to Scheduled Areas, empowers the Gram Sabha as the primary decision-making body.
  • Case studies: Jethipura Gram Panchayat (Gujarat) — Nanaji Deshmukh Rashtriya Gaurav Gram Sabha Puraskar, 2019 (special Gram Sabhas, Mahila Sabhas, quorum, SC/ST & women's participation); South Manubankul Gram Panchayat (Tripura) — recognised as a "women-friendly panchayat" by the Ministry of Panchayati Raj.
  • Quorum — the minimum number of members needed in an assembly meeting to make proceedings valid; Cooperatives — groups formed by people to work together for common benefit, sharing profits and responsibilities equally.
  • Participatory democracy — citizens' involvement beyond elections: local councils, public consultations, community-driven initiatives. Grassroots leaders: Chaudhary Charan Singh, Babu Jagjivan Ram (advocated for the rural poor and marginalised).
T10Challenges + Emergency (1975–77) ⚠️☐ revised
📖 Definition
Emergency — a special constitutional provision; Articles 352, 356 and 360 of the Constitution deal with National Emergency, President's Rule, and Financial Emergency respectively.
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Democracy ko maintenance chahiye! Challenges: illiteracy, fake news (social media se failti hai), poverty, regionalism, gender inequality, social discrimination, aur laws ke implementation me gaps. Sabse bada test tha Emergency 1975–77: Indira Gandhi ki government ke against bhaari protest (unemployment, inflation, misgovernance) → June 1975 me National Emergency lag gayi (ground: internal disturbance) → Fundamental Rights suspend, press censorship, neta activists jail me! Jayaprakash Narayan (Lok Nayak) ne Bihar-Gujarat me students aur citizens mobilise kiye. 1977 me Emergency hatai, elections hue, ruling government haari — proof ki democracy ki asli taqat: janta boss hai!
✍️ Exam Points
  • Democracy requires constant care, awareness, and active participation from both institutions and citizens.
  • Actions like damaging public property, spreading misinformation, or violating public rules weaken democratic values; indifference towards public issues also hurts democracy.
  • Emergency timeline: early 1970s — public dissatisfaction (rising unemployment, inflation, allegations of misgovernance) → June 1975: National Emergency imposed on grounds of internal disturbance → Fundamental Rights suspended, press censored, political leaders and activists arrested → mass movements led by Jayaprakash Narayan (Lok Nayak), especially in Bihar and Gujarat → 1977: Emergency lifted, general elections held → ruling government defeated.
  • The episode showed both the vulnerabilities and the resilience of Indian democracy — the power of the ballot.
  • Civic responsibility — respecting others' rights, using social media responsibly, abiding by laws, appreciating diversity, participating in activities that strengthen unity.
T11Democracy and You — Young Citizen's Toolkit 🎒☐ revised
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
Tum bhi democracy ka part ho! Formula: informed raho (newspaper, news, responsible internet use — fake news se door!), Constitution samjho, fundamental duties follow karo, school aur community me leadership lo, aur nation-building activities join karo — NSS, NCC, Bharat Scouts & Guides. Chhote steps bhi count karte hain — democracy sirf election day nahi, roz ka practice hai!
✍️ Exam Points — Before we move on (book summary)
  • Democracy is a system of self-governance involving institutions, rules and practices for participation at local, state and national levels — not limited to elections alone.
  • Democratic processes are guided by transparency, participation, inclusion, fairness, equality, accountability, and respect for diverse opinions.
  • Democratic ideas in India have deep roots in ancient traditions (Sabhā and Samiti in Vedic texts) — consultation and collective decision-making.
  • India's democracy rests on the Constitution: popular sovereignty, universal adult franchise, fundamental rights and duties, rule of law, separation of powers.
  • India is the world's largest democracy due to the scale of citizen participation — though challenges remain in deeper inclusion and representation.
  • Citizens contribute through civic awareness, responsible media use, community participation, and respect for democratic values in everyday life.
  • Programmes like NSS and NCC instil civic responsibility, social awareness and respect for democratic ideals.

📝Big Questions — Exam-Ready Answers (Ch 6)
Q1. What is democracy? Explain its meaning and foundational principles.
Answer
  • Democracy is a form of government in which the source of power and authority lies with the citizens, who elect their representatives through free and fair elections.
  • It is founded on the principles of freedom, equality, justice, rights and duties.
  • Citizens have the right to vote, express their opinions, and participate in decision-making processes.
  • India is the world's largest democracy — population over 140 crore and a voter base of over 96.8 crore (2024).
  • The Constitution guarantees and protects the Fundamental Rights of all citizens without discrimination based on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth.
Q2. Trace the evolution of democratic traditions in India from early times.
Answer
  • The idea of democracy has been part of the Indian ethos since early times — the Vedic assemblies Sabha, Samiti and Vidhata involved collective decision-making.
  • The king was not an independent ruler but worked in consultation with assemblies, ministers and officers.
  • The Aikyamatya Sūktam of the Ṛig Veda (10.191.3) emphasises collective thinking, shared discussion and unity of purpose.
  • The Bauddha Saṁghas established by Gautama Buddha encouraged debate and discussion; members could choose their leader and decide through voting.
  • Invasions and British colonisation disrupted political structures, but the independence struggle revived democratic ideas.
  • The Constituent Assembly (1946) drafted the world's longest written Constitution in 2 years, 11 months and 18 days.
Q3. Explain popular sovereignty and the rule of law as principles of democracy.
Answer
  • Popular sovereignty: the ultimate source of power lies with the people; the state derives its authority from them.
  • A government elected through free and fair elections formulates policies and implements laws.
  • Every citizen 18 years and above has the right to vote through a secret ballot — the Universal Adult Franchise.
  • Rule of law: ensures equality before the law and equal protection of the law; no one is above the law.
  • Disputes are resolved in courts through procedures established by law — not by force or influence; no citizen can be punished without due process.
  • Together they promote justice, accountability and public trust in the democratic system.
Q4. List the six Fundamental Rights given in the Constitution with their Articles.
Answer
  • Right to Equality — Articles 14–18.
  • Right to Freedom — Articles 19–22.
  • Right Against Exploitation — Articles 23–24.
  • Right to Freedom of Religion — Articles 25–28.
  • Cultural and Educational Rights — Articles 29–30.
  • Right to Constitutional Remedies — Article 32.
  • These rights are enforceable in courts of law, indivisible and inalienable, subject to reasonable restrictions; remedies can be sought under Articles 32 and 226; the Right to Education (Article 21A, added 2009) gives free and compulsory education to children aged 6–14.
Q5. What is the separation of powers? How does it maintain checks and balances?
Answer
  • Separation of powers distributes responsibilities among the legislature, executive and judiciary so that each functions effectively and responsibly.
  • The legislature makes laws, the executive implements them, and the judiciary interprets them — preventing the concentration of power in any one organ.
  • Example: Parliament can amend the Constitution, but the judiciary can review amendments for consistency with the Constitution.
  • The executive's enforcement powers are regulated by laws framed by Parliament and can be reviewed by courts.
  • If a law is inconsistent with constitutional provisions, the judiciary can declare it unconstitutional.
  • The judiciary also upholds rights through initiatives like Public Interest Litigation (PIL).
Q6. How is the government kept accountable and transparent in a democracy? Explain with examples.
Answer
  • Democratic governments are accountable to citizens through elections, public debates, and engagement with civil society.
  • Question Hour — the first hour of a parliamentary session — lets members question the government and hold it accountable.
  • The Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005 allows citizens to seek information from government agencies — a major transparency law.
  • Accountability bodies include the CAG, Central Information Commission (CIC), Lokpal and Central Vigilance Commission (CVC).
  • Media and civil society act as the fourth pillar, raising public issues and enabling freedom of expression.
  • Decentralisation through rural and urban local bodies ensures people's participation at the grassroots level.
Q7. Describe the different types of democracy practised around the world, with examples.
Answer
  • Direct democracy — citizens directly participate in most decision-making; some representative features exist; difficult in large countries — e.g., Switzerland.
  • Representative (indirect) democracy — people elect representatives; periodic elections; government accountable — e.g., India.
  • Parliamentary democracy — executive members are part of the legislature; executive accountable to the legislature; people elect the legislature — e.g., India, Canada (UK adds a constitutional monarchy).
  • Presidential democracy — executive independent of the legislature; President elected by the people and accountable to them — e.g., USA.
  • Different societies institutionalise democracy in diverse ways, depending on citizen participation and how political power is structured.
Q8. What makes India a 'living democracy'? Explain with facts and figures.
Answer
  • India's democracy stands out for its scale, depth and inclusiveness — nearly a hundred crore voters form an unparalleled exercise in public participation.
  • In 2024, India had over 96.8 crore registered voters — more than the total population of many countries.
  • On average, one Member of Parliament represents about 25 lakh people.
  • Elections are conducted in 22 scheduled languages; election symbols allow even those who cannot read to vote independently.
  • Over one million polling stations are set up, including remote mountains, forests, deserts and islands — sometimes for a single voter.
  • Over 2,800 registered political parties reflect India's social and regional diversity, letting many viewpoints compete peacefully.
  • A three-tier system (Union, State, Local), ADCs and the PESA Act extend democracy to the grassroots.
Q9. Compare the Indian experience of women's right to vote with that of Britain and the USA. What reservation exists in local bodies?
Answer
  • In many countries, women had to launch long struggles: in Britain, full voting rights came only in 1928; in the USA, in 1920, after decades of protests.
  • In contrast, when the Constitution came into force in 1950, Indian women were granted Universal Adult Franchise without any prolonged legal battle.
  • However, the right to vote does not automatically ensure equal participation — social prejudices still limit women's representation.
  • Article 243(d): not less than one-third of seats in every Panchayat are reserved for women; 21 States + 2 UTs provide 50% reservation.
  • Article 243(t): not less than one-third in every Municipality; as of 2023, 17 States + 1 UT have 50% in Urban Local Bodies.
  • Case studies like South Manubankul (Tripura) — the 'women-friendly panchayat' — show women's participation in action.
Q10. What challenges does Indian democracy face? Describe the Emergency of 1975–77.
Answer
  • Challenges include illiteracy, misinformation and fake news (especially via social media), poverty, regionalism, gender inequality and social discrimination.
  • Gaps in the implementation of laws and policies reduce public trust; damaging public property and indifference also weaken democracy.
  • Emergency background: in the early 1970s, public dissatisfaction with the Indira Gandhi government grew due to rising unemployment, inflation and allegations of misgovernance.
  • In June 1975, a National Emergency was imposed on grounds of internal disturbance — Fundamental Rights were suspended, the press censored, and political leaders and activists arrested.
  • Mass movements led by Jayaprakash Narayan ('Lok Nayak') mobilised students and citizens, especially in Bihar and Gujarat.
  • The Emergency was lifted in 1977; elections followed and the ruling government was defeated — proving the strength of the ballot and the resilience of Indian democracy.
  • Articles 352, 356 and 360 deal with National Emergency, President's Rule and Financial Emergency respectively.

T3Vidhan Parishad + Single Transferable Vote 🏛️☐ revised
💡 Samajh (Hinglish)
6 states me legislature ka "double floor" hai — Vidhan Parishad (Upper House, Legislative Council) + Vidhan Sabha (Lower House). Parishad ka composition interesting hai: Vidhan Sabha members + local bodies + graduates + teachers ke vote se, aur Governor ke nominated members (art, science, social service, literature, co-operatives ke log). Aur STV (Single Transferable Vote) ka logic: preference mark karo (1, 2, 3...), jo candidate quota poora kare wahi jeeta; sabse kam wala bahar, uske votes 2nd preference pe transfer!
🌳 System — Vidhan Parishad (Legislative Council) ka composition

    • By members of the Vidhan Sabha
    • By local bodies
    • By graduates and teachers of the State
  • Nominated members — by the Governor, from fields like art, science, social service, literature, and co-operatives
  • Bicameral states (6): Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh
  • Elections to the Vidhan Parishad use proportional representation by single transferable vote
✍️ Exam Points — STV ka process (steps)
  1. Voters mark their preferences (1st, 2nd, 3rd...) on the ballot.
  2. First preference votes are counted for all candidates; those who reach the quota (minimum votes needed to win) are declared elected.
  3. The candidate with the lowest votes is eliminated; their votes are transferred to the second preferences marked on those ballots.
  4. The process continues till the required number of candidates are elected.
🧮 Quota formula: Quota = Total valid votes ÷ (Seats to be filled + 1) — the minimum votes required to win.

T7ECI — 4 Main Functions 🛠️☐ revised

🌳 System — ECI Functions (detail me)

  • 4. Ensures free and fair elections
    • Inclusive initiatives for PwDs, senior citizens, transgender persons, PVTGs

  • The Speaker or Chairman of the House decides such cases.
  • India has a multi-party system — many parties exist and compete, reflecting the diversity of languages, cultures, religions and regions.
  • Defection can be seen as political opportunism — or as upholding conscience and responding to changing public expectations.

📝Big Questions — Exam-Ready Answers (Ch 7)
Q1. Why are elections essential in a democratic system?
Answer
  • Elections are one of the most important processes for exercising democratic rights — regular and periodic elections lie at the core of democracy.
  • They allow people to choose those who make decisions on their behalf; representatives are chosen through direct or indirect elections.
  • Elected representatives are accountable to the people — the right to elect and hold them accountable is ensured through periodic elections.
  • Our vote is the principal means of exercising this right.
  • Elections provide equality (every vote equal), representation (every voice counts), accountability (non-performers can be changed) and legitimacy (people's mandate).
  • A government cannot continue beyond its prescribed term without seeking the people's mandate again.
Q2. Explain the First-Past-The-Post system and proportional representation with examples.
Answer
  • FPTP (plurality system): each voter votes for one candidate in the constituency; the candidate with the most votes wins, even with less than 50% — e.g., in a 3-party contest, Party A with 40% forms the government.
  • We elect members of the Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabha using FPTP.
  • Proportional Representation: voters vote for a party, not a person; seats are allotted in proportion to total votes received — e.g., parties with 40%, 35%, 15%, 10% votes get seats in the same proportion.
  • Elections to the Rajya Sabha, the President and the Vice President use proportional representation (STV for the Vidhan Parishad/Rajya Sabha).
  • A majority system is the third type: a candidate needs more than 50%; otherwise the top two contest a second round.
Q3. What laws govern the conduct of elections in India? Explain their roles.
Answer
  • The Representation of the People Act, 1950 — deals mainly with the allocation of seats, delimitation of constituencies, and the preparation and revision of electoral rolls; ensures every citizen above 18 years has the right to vote without discrimination.
  • The Representation of the People Act, 1951 — covers all other aspects of the conduct of elections and post-election disputes: nomination of candidates, campaigns, voting procedures and dispute resolution.
  • It also lists electoral offences and corrupt practices: bribery; appealing for votes on religion, caste, race, community or language; using government personnel (gazetted officers, judges, armed forces, police, excise).
  • The Presidential and Vice-Presidential Act, 1952 also governs related elections.
  • Together these Acts ensure the integrity, transparency and accountability of India's electoral process.
Q4. What is delimitation? Why is it necessary?
Answer
  • Delimitation is the process of determining the number of seats and fixing the territorial boundaries in each state for elections to the Lok Sabha and the Vidhan Sabha.
  • It aims to keep the ratio of seats to population as equal as possible across all constituencies.
  • Boundaries of constituencies are not permanently fixed — they change with shifts in population density.
  • Without delimitation, one MP might represent five lakh people while another represents twenty-five lakh — defeating equal representation.
  • Article 82 mandates a Delimitation Commission; India has had four — in 1952, 1963, 1973 and 2002.
Q5. Describe the Election Commission of India and its functions.
Answer
  • The ECI is an autonomous, permanent constitutional body established on 25 January 1950; Articles 324–329 define its powers and duties.
  • The Constitution vests in the ECI the superintendence, direction and control of the entire election process.
  • It conducts elections to the Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha, Vidhan Sabha, Vidhan Parishad, the President and the Vice President.
  • Functions: (i) creates and revises electoral rolls (enumerators, SIR, claims & objections); (ii) decides election schedules considering weather, agricultural cycles, exams and festivals; (iii) registers political parties, allots symbols and classifies them (national/state/RUPP), acting as a quasi-judicial body in disputes; (iv) ensures free, fair and inclusive elections (PwDs, 85+ home voting, Braille EVMs, transgender and PVTG inclusion).
  • At the state level, the Chief Electoral Officer supervises election work; DMs, SDMs and Tahsildars serve as DEOs, ROs and EROs.
Q6. What is the Special Intensive Revision (SIR)? Why is it necessary?
Answer
  • SIR is an ECI exercise of updating, verifying and correcting the electoral rolls.
  • It ensures that no eligible citizen is left out and no ineligible person is included in the roll.
  • It adds all voters — especially young voters who have just turned 18 and may be left out due to lack of awareness.
  • It deletes names on the basis of death of the voter, change of residence, duplicate enrolment, and being permanently untraceable.
  • The ECI also gives time to raise claims and objections against the revised roll and settles them before publishing the final roll.
Q7. What role do political parties play in elections and democracy?
Answer
  • Political parties are essential to a democratic system — they work directly at the grassroots level.
  • They enable voters to make informed decisions during elections.
  • They organise public opinion, campaign on specific issues, and offer voters meaningful choices.
  • Voters learn about candidates, their policies, vision and positions on issues primarily through parties (rallies, manifestos, media debates).
  • The winning party forms the government — formulating and implementing policies and ensuring accountability; other parties form the opposition, participating in debates and committees.
  • They provide governance, accountability, representation and public engagement — helping democracy function effectively.
Q8. What is defection? Explain the Anti-Defection Law.
Answer
  • Defection — abandoning or switching from the political party under whose banner a candidate was elected; joining another party or acting independently of party decisions (like voting in the legislature).
  • Seen as political opportunism in the context of party discipline and stability — though it can also uphold conscience or respond to changing public expectations.
  • The Anti-Defection Law was passed in 1985 through the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act.
  • Purpose: prevent defection and provide stability to the elected government.
  • If a member of Parliament or a State Assembly voluntarily gives up party membership or votes against the party's whip, the member can be disqualified from the House.
  • The Speaker or Chairman of the House decides such cases.
Q9. How are political parties recognised as national or state parties? Mention the criteria.
Answer
  • The ECI registers and recognises political parties based on specific criteria.
  • National party (any one condition): secures not less than 6% of valid votes in four or more states plus wins 4 Lok Sabha seats; OR wins at least 2% of Lok Sabha seats with candidates elected from at least three states; OR is recognised as a state party in at least four states.
  • State party (any one condition): 6% of valid votes in the state Assembly election + at least 2 Assembly seats; OR 6% of LS votes from the state + 1 LS seat; OR at least 3% of Assembly seats (or 3 seats); OR 1 LS seat per 25 seats allotted to the state; OR 8% of valid votes polled in the state.
  • RUPP: registered-unrecognised parties — those with insufficient vote shares or that have never contested since registration.
  • National and state parties are allotted election symbols; the ECI insists on inner-party democracy (periodic organisational elections).
Q10. What are the challenges to free and fair elections? How does the ECI address them?
Answer
  • Conducting elections for over 96.8 crore voters across thousands of polling stations, hundreds of parties and diverse socio-economic realities is a challenging task.
  • Major challenges: misinformation, fake news, and intimidation of voters.
  • The ECI addresses them through the Representation of the People Acts (1950 and 1951) — laws covering offences and corrupt practices.
  • The Model Code of Conduct (MCC) regulates party behaviour during campaigns; EVMs and VVPAT (Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail) ensure transparent voting and counting.
  • Voters' awareness campaigns and inclusive initiatives (Braille EVMs, home voting for 85+ and PwDs, ETPBS for service voters) push the motto 'No Voter to Be Left Behind'.
  • With constant vigilance and active citizen participation, elections become more representative and democracy more robust.
